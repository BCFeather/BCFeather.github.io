The governing party in Ottawa chose its new leader in mid-March, and the choice, a former central banker who ran on the single promise of confronting the American tariff pressure head-on, reshaped the country's election campaign before it had properly begun. The new leader had never held elected office, had spent his career in the institutions of the financial world, and had entered the leadership race with the argument that the country's divisions, the ones that the previous government had spent its term managing, were secondary to the emergency from the south; the argument carried the party's membership, and the choice was the party's bet that the emergency framing would carry the country. The campaign that follows will be the test of the bet.

The new leader's background is the part of the story that the coverage has analysed most closely, because the background explains the appeal. The years at the head of the central bank, the management of the economy through the crises, and the international reputation that the financial world had built were the qualifications that the party's members were choosing, and the qualifications were the answer to the particular criticism that the government had faced: that it was not tough enough, not credible enough, not prepared for the fight that the American pressure had opened. The new leader's promise to fight back was the promise of the technician, the promise that the country's case would be made with the numbers and the nerve that the institutions had taught him, and the promise, the polls suggested, was landing.

The political positioning of the new leader is the part that the analysts will watch through the campaign. The promise to confront the American administration does the work that the traditional partisan appeals used to do: it unites the country behind the government, it makes the opposition's criticisms of the government's record harder to land, and it gives the campaign a theme that the voters can understand without the policy detail. The new leader has leaned on the theme with the discipline that the central banker's training suggests, and the discipline has kept the campaign on the message even as the events, the tariff deadlines, the negotiations, the carve-outs, have moved around it. The message is the strategy, and the strategy is the campaign.

The American administration's response to the Canadian choice was the part of the week that the coverage could not resist, and the response was delivered in the administration's characteristic register: the dismissal of the new leader, the repetition of the complaints about the trade relationship, and the insistence that the pressure would continue regardless of who leads the northern neighbour. The dismissal was received in Canada with the particular anger that the country has developed over the winter, and the anger is the new leader's most reliable asset; the American statements have done, at no cost to the Canadian campaign, the work of mobilising the electorate that the Canadian politicians could not have done on their own. The fight with the south has become the country's campaign, and both sides are supplying the material.

The election that the leadership choice sets up will be held within weeks, and the campaign will be short, intense and single-issue. The new leader will run on the promise of the resistance, the opposition will run on the record of the government and the costs of the confrontation, and the voters will choose between the two framings with the attention that the emergency has produced; the polls, in the days after the choice, gave the governing party its strongest numbers in years, and the numbers were the measure of the new leader's gift. Canada chose the banker who promised to fight back, and the choice has given the country the campaign that the winter prepared it for: the campaign about the relationship with the south, conducted in the language of the tariffs and the countermeasures, with the new leader's promise of the fight at the centre of it.
