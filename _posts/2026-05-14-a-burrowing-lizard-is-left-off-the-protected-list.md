On 4 May, a notice appeared in the Federal Register that almost no one outside a small circle herpetologists would read. The United States Fish and Wildlife Service announced that it had finished its review of a petition to protect the Temblor legless lizard, a thin, snake-like burrowing reptile found in central California, and had concluded that listing it under the Endangered Species Act was, for now, not warranted. To the untrained eye this was a quiet bureaucratic footnote. To anyone who has watched how American endangered-species policy actually works, it was a small and revealing case study.

The lizard itself is unglamorous. *Anniella alexanderae* lives in the loose, well-drained soils along the eastern foothills of the Temblor and Diablo ranges and the floor of the southern San Joaquin Valley, in a long, narrow strip roughly ninety miles long and eight miles wide that crosses parts of Kern, Kings, Fresno and San Luis Obispo counties. It burrows to depths of between one and twenty-four inches, hunts arthropods by listening for them through the soil, and needs the shade of shrubs and the moisture of ephemeral stream beds to get by. It is the kind of creature that does its entire life underground and out of sight.

The threats the agency identified are the familiar ones of the modern American West. Oil and gas development, with its drilling, pumping and fluid injection, removes habitat and sends vibrations through the ground that can drown out the lizard's prey-sensing. Spills of oil and wastewater can kill directly. Solar plants, orchards and row-crop agriculture convert the same scrubby land. And then there is the climate overlay: rising temperatures, deeper drought, more severe wildfires, all of which dry out the substrate the animal depends on. The service's own modelling acknowledged that under a high-development scenario, potentially suitable habitat would shrink from about 71 percent of the range to 58 percent by the end of the century.

So why not list it? The agency's reasoning turns on the statistical idea of resilience. The population size and growth rate are unknown, but the lizard is distributed across its entire range, can burrow deeper to escape heat, and can move along stream channels to recolonize patches disturbed by oil work. On the balance of the available evidence, the service judged, the species was not likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future across all of its range. The finding is an invitation to keep monitoring, not a declaration that the animal is safe.

That is the part of the system that is easy to miss from outside. Listing is not a single yes-or-no verdict but a slow rolling review, in which a species can be petitioned, assessed, declined, petitioned again as new information arrives. The agency explicitly invited the public to submit any new data on the lizard and its habitat at any time. A not-warranted finding in 2026 is a pause, not an acquittal, and the backlog of species awaiting exactly this kind of review is itself a quieter scandal than any single decision.

What the episode exposes is the mismatch at the heart of conservation triage. The work of deciding which small, obscure, underground animal warrants the full weight of federal protection is done in dense technical documents, on the basis of data that are often incomplete. The lizard's numbers are unknown because no one has yet studied them closely enough, and a decision made on thin data is, by construction, provisional. The lizard gets to stay unprotected for now. Whether that is wisdom or the limits of the system will be decided by the next review, whenever new information finally arrives.
