---
title: "The Wuhan University library case: an accusation and the slow work of institutions"
date: 2025-09-21 19:05:00 +0800
tags: [china, education, society, commentary]
description: The dispute between two students at Wuhan University became one of the most argued-about stories of the summer. After the courts and the university finished their work, the record turned out to be more complicated than anything that went viral.
---

For much of this summer, one of the loudest stories on the Chinese internet was a dispute between two students at Wuhan University. It began in 2023, when a female student, Yang, posted online that a male student, Xiao, had sexually harassed her while she studied in a campus library. The university gave Xiao a disciplinary sanction. He denied the allegation, and for a while the story faded. This summer it returned, retold so often that almost everyone had an opinion and almost no one had read the record.

The institutions, meanwhile, had been working through it slowly. Yang had sued Xiao, seeking damages over the alleged harassment. On 25 July a court in Wuhan dismissed her claims in full. She appealed; on 17 September the Wuhan Intermediate Court upheld the first-instance ruling. Three days later the university published the results of its own review. It revoked the sanction it had given Xiao, saying it respected the judicial outcome and that a review panel of three outside experts and two university members had gone through 91 sets of documents, interviewed 21 people and inspected the key locations and evidence.

The notice also dealt with the second question that had come to dominate the online debate: Yang's master's thesis, which critics had claimed was fraudulent. The university's experts found no plagiarism, no fabricated data and no invented conclusions; the raw data behind the paper could be checked. They did find more than a hundred problems, mostly in formatting, citation and translation, and the university conceded that the supervisor had not checked the thesis carefully and that the department had not reviewed it strictly. The degree stood. The supervisor was admonished and barred from taking on new graduate students for two years, three administrators received party disciplinary warnings, a deputy dean was admonished, and several departments were ordered to submit written self-criticism.

The same notice took the unusual step of correcting rumours that had travelled with the case. The thesis had not been named an excellent thesis, as was widely claimed. Xiao's studies had not been interrupted. The claim that Yang's uncle was an academician was false, and so were the claims that relatives of Xiao's had been badly harmed by online bullying, according to the university and the local authorities it consulted. Much of what people had been arguing about had never happened.

The case does not resolve into a single moral. The courts found against Yang's claims, and the university, respecting that, revoked the sanction on Xiao. But the same review found real problems on the other side: a thesis that had moved through the system too loosely, a supervisor who had not done his job, and an initial handling of a student dispute careless enough to draw disciplinary action against the university's own staff. The scrutiny that followed the case helped surface the thesis questions. It also produced a flood of claims that turned out to be false.

That is the part worth keeping in mind. Online, the case was settled within days, by people who had seen neither the evidence nor the proceedings. The institutions took months, and when they finished, their account was more complicated, and more honest, than anything that went viral. Certainty travelled faster than evidence in this case, which is how such cases usually go. The next time an accusation arrives and the answer already feels obvious, it is worth noticing how much of what we are sure about rests on nothing we have seen. The people who decided this case were the ones who had to look at the evidence. Everyone else was guessing from a distance.
