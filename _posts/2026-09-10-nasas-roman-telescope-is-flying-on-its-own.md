The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is on its own. On 30 August, about an hour after launching from Cape Canaveral on a Falcon Heavy, the spacecraft separated from the rocket's upper stage and began its solo flight toward the Sun-Earth L2 point, roughly a million miles beyond Earth. Within minutes of separation, it established contact with the ground through NASA's TDRS relay satellites, and over the following half hour it unfolded its solar panels and sunshield, the shield doing double duty as a power source and a heat protector for the instruments behind it. The launch itself was routine by the standards of Falcon Heavy; the parts that matter are the ones that come after, because Roman is not a telescope that does its job in a day.

Roman is often described as a successor to Hubble, and the description is roughly right in a way that misses the point. It has Hubble-class resolution, but its field of view is about a hundred times wider, which means it does not stare at single objects the way Hubble did. It surveys. In a single exposure it can cover an area of sky that would take Hubble months to cross, and over its planned five-year mission, it is expected to map billions of galaxies. That design makes it a census instrument: the science questions it is built to answer, about dark energy, dark matter and the population of planets around other stars, are questions that only make sense with enormous samples.

The scientific division of labour with the James Webb Space Telescope, which arrived at L2 four and a half years ago, is worth spelling out. Webb looks deep and narrow; Roman looks wide and comparatively shallow. The two are complementary in a practical way: Roman finds the faint signatures and the statistical patterns, and Webb follows up on the objects that deserve a closer look. Astronomers have spent years planning exactly this handoff, and the two spacecraft will spend their overlapping years at L2 trading targets. L2 is becoming crowded in a pleasant way; the region has room for a fleet, and the ground stations that support it are being shared across agencies, with the ESA and JAXA networks helping to relay Roman's data.

The mission has already survived its first and riskiest hour, the one that separates deployment from discovery. The shield that unfolded on 30 August is what allows the instruments to cool to the temperatures they need, and the same mechanism protects the telescope from the Sun's glare. NASA's engineers will spend the next weeks checking systems, and the first science images are not expected until late this year or early next. The telescope will then begin the survey that gives the mission its scientific identity: a wide-field map of the sky in infrared, repeated over time, which is how the expansion history of the universe and the behaviour of dark energy are measured.

The parts of this story that get told loudly are the launch and the numbers, and they are real: a flagship observatory, a decade and a half in development, a $4 billion price tag, and now a working spacecraft on its way to L2. The part that gets told quietly is what the survey actually means. Dark energy is the name given to the observation that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, and the entire physics of that acceleration is a placeholder. Roman's wide field is the experiment: measure the expansion at a million points, and the pattern either fits the standard model or it does not. The same applies to exoplanets, where Roman's microlensing survey is expected to find thousands of new worlds, many of them too small and too cold for other methods.

There is also a longer arc worth noting. Roman was named after Nancy Grace Roman, the NASA astronomer who pushed for a space telescope in the 1960s and who was told for years that the idea was too ambitious. The mission carrying her name is the institutional descendant of that persistence. The telescope that is flying toward L2 now will do its work in wide sweeps of the sky rather than deep gazes, and the pattern it finds will be the kind that takes a census to see. Hubble answered questions one object at a time; Roman is built to answer them one billion at a time. The spacecraft is still on its way, and the science is still ahead of it, but the argument that a survey telescope deserves its place in the flagship fleet has already been settled.
