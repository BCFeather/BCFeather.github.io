---

Solid-state lithium metal batteries are the most cited next-generation energy storage technology, and for good reason: a lithium metal anode paired with a solid electrolyte promises roughly twice the energy density of today's lithium-ion cells, with no flammable liquid to leak or catch fire. For a decade the field has pushed ionic conductivity ever higher, and modern solid electrolytes now rival liquid ones. The bottleneck that remains is mechanical, not chemical. The rigid interface between a lithium metal anode and a brittle solid electrolyte fractures under the volume changes of plating and stripping, and once cracks form, resistance spikes and short circuits follow.

The paper published in *Nature* on 30 October 2025, led by a team at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School with collaborators, attacks that fracture problem directly. Instead of making the electrolyte stiffer, the authors design the interphase itself to be ductile. The key is a silver-based layer formed *in situ*: when a silver nitrate additive in a composite polymer electrolyte contacts the lithium metal anode, a substitution reaction produces silver sulfide and silver fluoride, which form a mixed interphase rich in these two compounds. The silver sulfide is intrinsically ductile at room temperature, and the silver fluoride adds stiffness and ionic conductivity. The result is an interphase that deforms rather than cracks.

The numbers matter. The ductile interphase survives prolonged cycling at high current densities and high areal capacities, the conditions under which conventional solid-state cells fail fastest. The composite electrolyte reaches ionic conductivities around one millisiemens per centimetre, which is already competitive, but the contribution that is new is the interface stability. The authors show that the interphase maintains contact with the lithium anode across hundreds of cycles without the void formation and contact loss that typically plague solid-state cells. Operando microscopy and post-mortem analysis trace the improvement to the ductile layer flowing to fill gaps as lithium is consumed and redeposited.

The mechanism is worth dwelling on because it reframes the design rule. The conventional approach has been to seek an electrolyte that is hard enough to suppress lithium dendrites while being soft enough to maintain contact, a contradiction that no single material has resolved. The ductile interphase sidesteps the trade-off by separating the functions. The electrolyte handles bulk ionic transport; the interphase at the anode handles contact and mechanical compliance. This division of labour is familiar from liquid electrolytes, where the solid-electrolyte interphase naturally forms a ductile passivation layer on graphite anodes, but it has been difficult to engineer deliberately in solid systems.

The work is not a product announcement. The cell formats demonstrated are small laboratory coins, and the silver-based interphase adds cost that a commercial cell would need to justify against energy density gains. The team is explicit that scale-up, long-cycle testing under practical stack pressure, and compatibility with high-voltage cathodes are all open questions. What the paper establishes is a design principle: the interphase, not just the bulk electrolyte, is the place to engineer ductility.

A decade of solid-state battery progress has been bottlenecked by a problem that sounds trivial, the metal and the ceramic refusing to stay in contact. A layer that bends instead of cracking is the kind of unsexy materials solution that moves a field forward.
