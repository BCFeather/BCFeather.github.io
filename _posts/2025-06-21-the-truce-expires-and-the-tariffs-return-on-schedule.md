The thirty-day truce that had opened the trade conflict's most hopeful chapter expired on its scheduled date in the third week of June, and the tariffs returned on schedule: the suspension lifted, the rates restored, and the negotiations that the window had opened left, for the moment, without the agreement that would have made the pause permanent. The expiration was conducted with the official statements that the year has made familiar, the announcements that the talks would continue and the positions that had prevented the settlement, and the markets absorbed the resumption with the adjustment that the year's pattern has trained them to make. The window closed without the settlement, and the conflict resumed with the costs that the spring had measured.

The negotiations' outcome, through the thirty days, is the part that the analysts have been weighing since the expiration. The technical sessions had produced the mapping of the sectors and the rates, the ministerial calls had exchanged the positions, and the draft texts had been circulated and revised; the political terms, the conditions that the two governments' red lines had fixed, had not been bridged, and the bridging's absence was the window's failure. The thirty days were the test of whether the escalation's costs had produced the flexibility that the settlement required, and the test's answer, delivered by the expiration, was the one that the spring's pattern had made likely: the costs were real, and the flexibility was still short of the settlement's requirements.

The resumption's effects will be measured through the summer, and the measuring will begin with the markets' adjustment. The sectors that the truce had relieved were repriced on the resumption, the currencies and the bonds moved with the trade's new arithmetic, and the analysts' models were updated with the rates that the expiration had restored; the costs that the spring had documented, the prices, the volumes, the growth forecasts, will accumulate through the summer, and the accumulation will be the pressure that the next round of the talks will operate under. The resumption is the status quo of the conflict, and the status quo's costs are the variable that the politics on both sides will have to weigh.

The politics of the resumption are the part that will determine the conflict's next turn. The two governments have framed the expiration in the languages that their domestic audiences require, the administration citing the partner's insufficient concessions and the partner citing the administration's unreasonable demands, and the framings have left the door open for the talks' resumption through the channels that the year has maintained; the next pause, if it comes, will be the product of the costs' accumulation and the politics' calculation, and the calculation will be conducted through the summer's events. The conflict's rhythm has been established, and the rhythm's next beat will be set by the negotiations that the expiration has not ended.

The truce's meaning, in retrospect, will be the question that the summer's analysis will keep returning to. The thirty days demonstrated that the two sides could talk, that the technical work could proceed and that the pauses could be declared; the window's closure demonstrated that the talking had not yet produced the settlement, and that the positions remained far enough apart to absorb the window's promise. The truce was the trade conflict's most hopeful chapter, and the chapter's end was the return to the costs that the spring had measured; the return is the conflict's default, and the default will hold until the costs' accumulation and the politics' calculation produce the next window, and the next window's fate will be decided by the same forces that decided this one: the positions that neither side has been able to move, and the costs that both sides have been able to measure.
