In the last week of the holidays the stationer's shop becomes the busiest place in the neighbourhood. Tables of notebooks appear on the pavement, boxes of pencils stand open at the door, and the queue inside moves slowly past shelves of erasers, rulers and coloured pens. The season is not announced by the calendar. It is announced by the appearance, out of nowhere, of a display of exercise books, ruled and squared, stacked by the hundred.

The ritual is older than the shops themselves. A new term has always required new things, and the list the school sends home is a small economy of its own. Notebooks of a specified size, pens of a specified width, a geometry set, a glue stick, a packet of dividers. The prices are modest, the quantities are many, and by the time a family has worked through the list the bag is heavy and the wallet is lighter. Nobody enjoys the bill. Almost everyone enjoys the buying.

That pleasure is the curious part. The stationer's in these weeks is a kind of theatre of the future. A fresh notebook, blank and ruled, is a promise that has not yet been broken. It holds the possibility of a year kept neatly, a page a day, no smudges, no torn corners. The buyer knows, because every previous year proved it, that the same notebook will by November be dog-eared, half-filled and stained. In August, though, that knowledge is suspended. The book is still perfect, and so, briefly, is the year it stands for.

The shop itself runs on this suspension. It stocks the bright-coloured folders, the mechanical pencils that click, the pen holders shaped like animals, because the point is not function but the feeling of beginning. A child choosing among twelve shades of notebook cover is not really buying stationery. They are rehearsing the person they mean to be when term starts, the organized version, the one who does not lose the lunchbox and hands homework in on time. The purchase is a small investment in that person.

There is something gentle about how uncommercial this commerce remains. The big retailers have their back-to-school campaigns, but the real business still happens in the narrow shop on the corner, where the owner knows the list by heart and suggests the right pen before the parent has finished reading it out. The trade is small and slow and human, and it repeats every year regardless of the rest of the economy. While children go to school, this shop will have its August fortnight.

The deeper thing being marked is a transition that the summer otherwise blurs away. The holidays are a long, unstructured drift, and the stationer's is the first firm signal that the drift is ending. The notebooks on the pavement are the world's way of saying that routines are returning, that the late mornings and the long afternoons outside are numbered. For the child this is a small grief. For the adult accompanying them, it is a quieter relief.

By the time the term actually begins, the notebooks will already be partly used, the pens will be uncapped, the first mistakes will be inked on the fresh pages. The promise of August will have met the ordinary business of September. None of that diminishes these last shopping afternoons. To buy a new notebook is to buy, for a few minutes, the version of the year in which everything still goes right.
