At the opening ceremony of the 76th International Astronautical Congress in Sydney on 29 September, the team behind the Chang'e 6 mission received the International Astronautical Federation's World Space Award, the highest honour the field's global body bestows. The award went to the mission that, in the summer of 2024, landed on the far side of the Moon, scooped and drilled samples from its surface, and returned them to Earth: the first samples ever brought back from the Moon's hidden hemisphere, and one of the defining achievements of the current lunar era.

The significance of the mission is worth restating, because it is easy to compress into a phrase and lose. Every previous lunar sample return had come from the near side; the far side, facing away from Earth, is geologically a different world, with different crust, different history, and no direct line of sight to the home planet, which makes every step of a mission there harder, including communication. Chang'e 6 overcame all of it, through a relay satellite, precise landing in the South Pole-Aitken basin, and a return that completed the journey. The samples have since been studied by teams in China and abroad, and they have already begun to answer questions about the far side's volcanic history and its composition.

The award also lands at a particular moment in the lunar calendar. The programme that produced Chang'e 6 is part of a wider national effort that has established China as one of the two or three actors shaping the return to the Moon: the sample returns, the rover missions, the plans for a crewed landing and a permanent station. The international recognition is therefore not a solitary honour; it is a marker of how the field views the trajectory of the programme that produced it.

The ceremony's setting added a layer of meaning. The congress was held in Australia, a country whose own space ambitions are growing, and the award was delivered in the presence of the global community the congress gathers, the agencies, companies and laboratories that will build the next phase of exploration. An award at such a gathering is read by the field as a statement about which achievements the era will be remembered by, and the far side of the Moon is near the top of that list.

The team's members accepted the award on behalf of a mission that involved thousands of people across years, and the acceptance was, in the way of such occasions, a formality covering a mountain of work. The mission's engineering, the landing, the sampling, the ascent from the far side, the rendezvous and the return, each step had to work the first time, with no margin for the kind of failure that a near-side mission might survive. The World Space Award is a recognition that the field confers sparingly; Chang'e 6 earned it by doing something no one had done, returning a piece of the Moon's other side to the laboratories of Earth. In Sydney, in the last days of September, the achievement was placed among the best the space age has produced, and there is no arguing with the placement.
