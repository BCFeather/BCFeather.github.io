The first working week of 2025 closed with the year already in motion, and the motion was visible in the small print rather than the headlines. The markets opened the year with a cautious sell-off and recovered by Friday, which is the pattern that nervous years usually announce in their first five trading days. The courts published their calendars for the winter, and the calendars contained the cases that the country will spend the year arguing about. And the political calendar moved, steadily and without surprises, toward the inauguration that will dominate the month and set the tone for the year. The year did not begin with a bang; it began with a schedule, and the schedule was the news.

The market's first week deserves a closer look than the indexes usually get. The sell-off on the first trading day was attributed, in the business pages, to the usual suspects, the rates, the valuations, the year-end profit-taking that never quite ends, and the recovery by Friday was attributed to the same causes read backwards. The week's real content was the quiet repricing of risk that happens whenever a new year forces investors to look at the same numbers with fresh eyes, and the numbers said what they have been saying for months: the economy is growing, the rates are high, and the distance between the two facts is where the arguments live.

The court calendar for the winter is the part of the week that the coverage will regret underplaying. The cases scheduled for the first months of the year include the appeals that will decide the fate of the major enforcement actions, the challenges to the regulatory rules, and the first hearings in the disputes that the previous year's laws created. The courts do not announce their importance; they announce their dates, and the dates are the quiet infrastructure on which the year's politics will run. The lawyers and the lobbyists know this, which is why their calendars are already full.

The political schedule, meanwhile, has the rigidity of a countdown. The days between the first week and the inauguration are occupied by the ceremonies and the formalities that a transfer of power requires, the certifications, the farewells, the transitions of the agencies, and the coverage of each one will carry the same question, which is what the new administration will actually do with the power it is about to receive. The question will be answered unevenly: some things in the first hundred days, some things in the first hundred hours, and some things not at all, and the schedule of the answers will itself become a story.

The first week of January is the annual reminder that the world runs on calendars rather than on impulses. The markets will trade on sentiment and the politicians will campaign on emotion, and underneath both, the schedulers, the clerks, the docket keepers, will move the year forward one date at a time. The heavy year ahead was already fully booked before it began, and the first working week was the proof: five days, a small sell-off, a recovery, and a calendar that the rest of the year will spend catching up to.
