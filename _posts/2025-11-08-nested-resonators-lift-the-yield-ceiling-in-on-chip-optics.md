Integrated photonics has a quiet production problem. The chips that route, convert and generate light are built by the same lithography as electronics, and like electronics they are subject to nanometre-scale variations from wafer to wafer and run to run. For most components those variations are tolerable. For nonlinear devices they are fatal: frequency conversion demands that light of different wavelengths stay in phase as they travel, a condition so sensitive that a thickness difference of a few nanometres can destroy it. The result has been a frustrating asymmetry, a technology that works beautifully in the laboratory and fails unpredictably on the production line. A paper in the 6 November issue of *Science* proposes a way to break that asymmetry by changing what the device is asked to do.

The scheme, reported by a team including researchers at NIST and collaborators, is called nested frequency-phase matching. The key move is to stop trying to make every ring resonator satisfy the phase-matching condition exactly, which no fabrication process can guarantee, and instead to arrange a lattice of coupled resonators with two deliberately different timescales. Because the lattice as a whole contains a range of resonances, some pair of wavelengths in the structure will always find matching conditions somewhere, and the conversion proceeds passively, without the strict geometry that single devices require. It is a solution that works by embracing the disorder that manufacturing cannot remove, rather than by demanding it vanish.

The demonstration is striking for its completeness. The team fabricated lattices of silicon-nitride coupled ring resonators in a commercial foundry, with no special treatment, and generated light in the fundamental, second, third and fourth harmonic bands simultaneously, an ultrabroadband output spanning visible and near-infrared frequencies from a single passive chip. Where conventional nonlinear devices on a wafer might yield a small fraction of working units, the nested lattice achieved a 100% functional yield across the wafer, and the harmonics were produced with no geometry fine-tuning at all. Distinct spatial and spectral signatures confirmed that the mechanism was the intended one.

The practical significance is easier to see than the physics. Photonics is in the middle of a commercial build-out, driven by data-centre interconnects, sensing and the promise of optical computing, and the industry needs components that can be made in volume at competitive cost. A nonlinear chip that works on the first try, every time, on a standard foundry line, removes one of the main barriers between laboratory photonics and industrial photonics. Frequency conversion, modulation and broadband light generation become manufacturing-friendly rather than experimental luxuries.

As with any new scheme, the details will be tested and the limits mapped: the yield figure is exceptional, but the conversion efficiencies, power handling and tunability of the nested lattices will determine how far the approach extends. What is already clear is the conceptual turn. The field has spent decades designing devices that must be perfect and then fighting fabrication for the privilege. This work quietly inverts the task, designing the disorder in from the start and letting the array find its own phase matching. The wafer, imperfect as ever, is no longer the enemy but part of the instrument.
