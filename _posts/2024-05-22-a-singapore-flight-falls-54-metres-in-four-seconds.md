On 21 May, a Singapore Airlines flight from London to Singapore ran into turbulence so severe that it stopped being an abstraction. Flight SQ321, a Boeing 777-300ER carrying 211 passengers and 18 crew, was cruising at about 11,300 metres over Myanmar when the aircraft dropped roughly 1,900 metres in three minutes, including one plunge of about 54 metres in 4.6 seconds, a fall that threw unbelted passengers towards the ceiling and then back down. The captain diverted to Bangkok, landing at 15:45 local time, and one passenger, a 73-year-old British man, died, apparently of a heart attack; dozens were injured, several seriously, with head injuries common among those who had not been strapped in.

The sequence is now well documented. The cockpit's weather radar had flagged developing storms ahead, and the pilots had turned on the seatbelt sign within eleven seconds of the first sign of convection, according to the preliminary investigation released by Singapore's transport ministry on 29 May. But turbulence of this kind, called clear-air turbulence in aviation shorthand, is invisible to radar, and the cabin was caught mid-service: trolleys in the aisles, trays on tables, passengers up and moving. The crew had not had time to secure the cabin before the gravity changed, and the difference between those who survived unhurt and those who did not was largely the difference between having a belt on and not having one.

What makes this accident worth pausing over is not its rarity but its ordinariness. Turbulence is the most common cause of injury in commercial flying, and it is among the least preventable. Airlines have spent decades improving engines, navigation and maintenance, while the atmosphere itself has become a harder environment: studies published in recent years, and cited again in the aftermath of this crash, point to a warming climate strengthening the jet streams and increasing the frequency of the clear-air turbulence that radar cannot see. The accident is a reminder that the safest form of travel still depends, at its margins, on a factor no engineer has yet designed out.

The response has been a small flurry of policy and procedure. Singapore Airlines said it would stop serving hot meals and drinks whenever the seatbelt sign is on, and began reviewing its turbulence procedures; the industry debate has reopened around whether seatbelt signs should be on for the whole flight, and whether passengers should be required to remain seated. These are modest measures, and they are worth supporting, but they push the burden of safety onto the passenger, which is exactly where the industry has always been reluctant to push it.

The deeper lesson of the accident is about the asymmetry of risk perception. A plane that falls 54 metres sounds terrifying, and it is; yet the passenger who died did so of a heart attack, and the hundreds of other passengers walked off the aircraft. Flying remains extraordinarily safe, and the probability of any individual flight ending in an incident like this is vanishingly small. What the accident should change is not whether people fly, but how they behave in the seat: the belt fastened, the tray down, the drinks in the overhead bin. The physics of the atmosphere will not be legislated away, but the arithmetic of injury can be changed by a buckle, in four and a half seconds, for anyone who happens to be sitting in the wrong part of the cabin at the wrong moment.
