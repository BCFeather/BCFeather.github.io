title: "From martial law to the dock: a president's year on trial"
date: 2025-09-28 11:40:00 +0800

description: Six hours of martial law in December 2024 led to impeachment, arrest, removal and a criminal trial that is still running. How South Korea has handled the accusation against Yoon Suk Yeol, and why the slow part matters.
---

A dispute inside a university library in Wuhan became one of the most talked-about stories on the Chinese internet this summer. A female student, surnamed Yang, had posted back in 2023 that a male student, surnamed Xiao, sexually harassed her while she studied there. The university sanctioned Xiao; he denied everything and sued. Much of the public had long since made up its mind, and most of it sided with Xiao. The courts took longer. A first-instance ruling went in his favour, and on 20 September a second-instance ruling upheld it, the same day the university revoked the sanction and reported that it had also reviewed the student's thesis. Whatever one makes of the details, the episode was a reminder of how quickly people settle on a verdict about an accusation, and how slowly institutions are meant to move.

South Korea has spent the past year working through a far larger accusation, and the contrast is instructive. Yoon Suk Yeol, the president who declared martial law on the night of 3 December 2024, has not been judged in the comment sections. He has been judged step by step, by parliament, by prosecutors, by a constitutional court, and now by a criminal court whose sessions run to the end of the year. The process has been messy, contested and political at every turn. That may be exactly why it matters.

The night itself was brief. Shortly after ten in the evening, Yoon appeared on television and declared emergency martial law, citing what he called anti-state forces threatening to paralyse the country. The decree banned the National Assembly, political parties and public gatherings, and placed the media under the control of the martial law command. For a few hours soldiers and police moved towards the Assembly building, and helicopters set down on its grounds. Then the Assembly defied the order. Lawmakers climbed and slipped past the cordon to reach the chamber, and voted 190 to zero to lift the decree. Yoon's government accepted the vote within hours, and by early morning the crisis was over, six hours after it began. It was the first martial law in South Korea in forty-five years.

The aftermath moved at a different pace. On 14 December the National Assembly impeached Yoon, suspending his powers. On 31 December a court issued an arrest warrant, the first ever against a sitting South Korean president. On 15 January investigators entered his residence and took him into custody; eleven days later he was indicted, charged as the suspected ringleader of an insurrection. He was released from detention in March after a court granted his request, and on 4 April the Constitutional Court unanimously upheld the impeachment, making him a former president. An early presidential election followed in June, and the opposition won it.

The criminal trial that began in April has become the longest chapter. Prosecutors and a special counsel team argue that the martial law decree was a plan to seize control of the state, that troops were meant to drag lawmakers out of the chamber, that arrest lists were drawn up, and that the emergency was manufactured. Yoon's defence tells a different story: a warning, a display of resolve against a parliament that had crippled his government, a decree that ended quickly because it was always meant to, and a prosecution driven by political enemies. The courtroom has heard witnesses on both sides. Yoon stayed away through most of the summer, skipping ten consecutive hearings on health grounds, then appeared on 26 September for the first time in eighty-five days, denied the charges, and listened as the court began questioning key witnesses. Fifteen further sessions are scheduled before December.

The two accounts cannot both be true, and deciding which one holds is the court's job. What has been striking so far is how much of the system held up under the weight of the case. The Assembly voted within hours on a night when soldiers were outside its doors. The Constitutional Court reached its decision while large parts of the public were furious either way. The criminal trial continues even though the defendant is a former head of state. None of this was smooth, and South Korean politics is more polarised now than it was before the crisis. But the machinery that was supposed to process the accusation did not break.

It is tempting, in cases like this, to treat the crowd as the jury. The Wuhan episode showed how far that instinct can run, and how often it is wrong. The Korean case shows the alternative: an accusation taken seriously, tested over many months, and answered by people whose job is to weigh evidence rather than to entertain. A president who once moved against his own parliament now sits through hearings as an ordinary defendant. That, more than any single verdict, is the point worth keeping. Institutions that make even the powerful wait are easy to mock when they are slow. They are also the only ones that leave room for the truth to be established, and for the record to show, later, that it was.
