While the week was dominated by speculation about what the biggest central banks will do next, a smaller one made a quiet and revealing decision. On 20 October, the Monetary Council of Hungary's central bank left its base rate unchanged at 5.50 per cent, holding the overnight deposit and lending rates at 4.50 and 6.50 per cent as well, with effect from 21 October. Unremarkable on the surface, it is a decision worth reading against the backdrop the bank itself described.

The puzzle is the inflation number. In September, consumer-price inflation stood at 1.6 per cent, with core inflation at 1.9 per cent, both in line with the bank's own forecast and comfortably below the 2.5 per cent target that Hungary will formally adopt from the start of 2028. Food prices were still falling. By the arithmetic of inflation alone, a central bank this close to target, in a region where Romania and Poland also held steady in October, has room to ease. The council chose not to.

The reasons sit outside the domestic data. The bank pointed to high and still-rising long-term yields across developed markets, a stronger US dollar, and debt-sustainability worries that are weighing on investor sentiment. It noted that, based on market pricing, the European Central Bank, the Federal Reserve and even the Bank of Japan may raise again after their September moves. With global oil and gas prices elevated on geopolitical tension, the inflation outlook is skewed the wrong way, and the bank expects domestic inflation to climb to 3.1 per cent next year on higher fuel costs and a rise in tobacco taxes. Cutting now, into that setup, would risk the currency and the country's risk premium.

That trade-off is the central fact of monetary policy in a smaller open economy this autumn. Hungary has positive real interest rates and low unemployment, and real wages are growing strongly, so the domestic case for stimulus exists. But the bank has to set policy not for Hungary alone but for a small economy borrowing in a world where US yields and the dollar are both moving against it. Lowering rates while the big central banks lean on tight policy would pressure the forint and, through imported prices, undo the very disinflation the bank has worked for.

The bank's language was careful: maintaining the current rate setting is what is needed to hit the target sustainably, and future decisions will be data-driven and cautious. It explicitly flagged that fiscal policy, expectations around euro adoption and the external market environment will shape Hungary's risk assessment. In other words, the rate decision is hostage to politics and global yields as much as to the inflation print.

The broader lesson is that the autumn's central-bank story is not being written in Washington. Across the region, rate-setters are holding in a higher-for-longer world, tolerating below-target inflation at home because the global cost of easing is judged higher. For Hungarian savers and borrowers, the practical effect is a continuation of expensive credit through the winter. For anyone watching the next move, the abridged minutes are due on 4 November, and the data between now and then will decide whether this hold is the last pause before a cut, or the first in a long wait.
