In the first week of October the country performs its most visible annual ritual: the National Day holiday, the Golden Week, when the working year pauses and tens of millions of families, in one coordinated movement, take to the roads, the railways and the airports. The scale is hard to hold in the head. The numbers are announced after the fact in tens of millions of trips, and the figures are so large that they cease to mean anything except one thing: the biggest shared holiday in the largest society on Earth is also its most impressive feat of logistics, executed every year, mostly without incident, under the eyes of everyone taking part.

The experience of the holiday is, for the traveller, a study in the physics of crowds. The stations fill; the platforms acquire a density that would alarm any engineer of pedestrians; the scenic spots that photographs show serene in the off-season appear in October as a sea of heads. The queues form for tickets, for entry, for food, for the washroom, and the queue is the holiday's true unit of experience. And yet the remarkable thing is not that the queues form; it is that they move, that the system absorbs the surge, that the trains add carriages and the high-speed network stretches and the country's enormous machinery of movement handles, every year, the weight of its people on holiday.

The sociology of the Golden Week is worth a moment's attention. The holiday is a concentrated expression of several things at once: the economic rise that has made leisure travel normal, the family structure that sends the generations out together, the sheer curiosity about the country itself, the famous sites, the mountains, the coast, the other provinces. The destinations change over the years, but the pattern is stable: the country, given a week off, chooses to see itself, in one great simultaneous tour.

There are criticisms of the week that are also true: the crowds, the prices, the sense that one has seen the attraction through the backs of other people's heads. The criticisms are accurate and beside the point. A society that can fill every scenic spot in the country for a week and still function, still feed itself, still move everyone home again, has demonstrated something its citizens rarely stop to notice: the sheer capacity of the system they live in. The holiday is not only a break from work; it is, at scale, a display of the country's organisational muscle.

The week ends, as it always does, with the great return, and the stations empty back into the cities, and the numbers are announced, and the ordinary rhythm resumes. The Golden Week is over for another year, and the queues have been dissolved back into the daily life that produced them. But the memory of the holiday, the crowds and the mountains and the family meals, stays, and so does the quiet fact beneath it: a whole society, given the week, chose to move, and the movement, improbably, worked. The biggest shared holiday in the world is also, every October, one of its most successful shared operations.
