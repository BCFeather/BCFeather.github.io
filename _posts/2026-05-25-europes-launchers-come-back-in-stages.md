With the tenth Ariane 6 launch in April and Vega-C's return to operational service in May, Europe has quietly reached the launch cadence it promised when it designed its new rocket family, and the milestone deserves more attention than it is getting. The decade between Ariane 5's retirement and now was one long lesson in what happens when a space power stops launching on its own terms: commercial customers drifted to cheaper American and Chinese vehicles, institutional payloads waited in line, and the political case for European access to space was made by the absence of it. The recovery of the last year has been real, and it is worth examining on its own terms rather than through the lens of the companies that are winning elsewhere.

The numbers are more encouraging than the narrative. Ariane 6 has now flown a mix of institutional and commercial payloads, including the first batch of European navigation satellites launched on the new vehicle, and its upper stage, the part that caused the original delay, has performed cleanly through all ten flights. Vega-C, the smaller rocket designed for the medium market, returned with a European Earth-observation satellite in May, its first flight since the in-flight failure that grounded it for eighteen months. The fleet is no longer a promise; it is a schedule.

What the schedule cannot paper over is the market it has returned to. When Ariane 6 was designed, Europe expected to compete with one or two rivals for a growing commercial market. It now competes with a reusable American vehicle that has made price per kilogram a commodity, with Chinese launch services that bundle rideshare aggressively, and with a commercial market that has learned to treat launch as an interchangeable service. The European response, a fixed-price contract for the vehicle's early years and a reusable version under study, is sensible and slow at the same time, which is a fair summary of how the continent does everything in space.

I am, on balance, more hopeful than the headlines. The argument for European launch capacity was never purely economic; it was about the ability to put European satellites in orbit without asking for permission, and that argument has not weakened. The ten Ariane 6 flights have rebuilt the industrial base, the workforce and the confidence that were dissolving during the gap, and those are assets that compound even when the market is brutal. The continent's problem was never that it could not build rockets; it was that it forgot how to fly them regularly, and regularity is exactly what the last twelve months have restored.

The harder test comes next year, when the institutional backlog thins out and Ariane 6 must win commercial business against a rocket that lands itself. No amount of patriotism will close that gap if the price is not competitive, and Europe knows it: the reusable studies are funded, the production line is being rationalized, and the operators are talking like businessmen rather than ministers. That is progress of a kind that does not make the front page, and it is the kind that matters most for a programme whose real competitors are time and the cost curve.
