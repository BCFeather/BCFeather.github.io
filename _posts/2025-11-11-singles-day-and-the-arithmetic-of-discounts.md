On the eleventh day of the eleventh month, the largest shopping festival on the planet conducts its annual business, and a remarkable fraction of it is arithmetic. The prices look straightforward, but behind them stands a structure of pre-sale reservations, red packets, stacked coupons, platform subsidies and threshold-crossing bonuses, each with its own conditions, each valid in a different window, each requiring a calculation of its own. For several weeks every autumn, the ordinary act of buying something becomes a test of number sense, and the numbers are arranged by the seller.

The discounts are real, in the narrow sense that goods do get cheaper. Supply chains adjust, brands clear inventory, platforms subsidise traffic, and a shopper who plans carefully can buy a washing machine or a phone for less than it cost last month. None of this is a lie. What the arithmetic obscures is the distribution of the gains. The savings are concentrated where they can be seen: on popular items, in the first hours, in the categories where the discounts are deepest. Elsewhere the festival is a re-labelling, prices raised quietly before being discounted, bundles assembled from slow-moving stock, and small purchases swept up in the celebration of large ones. The careful arithmetic, applied diligently by the buyer, is applied much more diligently by the seller, who has the data on what each number does to behaviour.

The deeper effect is on judgement, and it is not the effect the discounts advertise. A person does not usually save money by deciding to spend more, but the festival's structure makes the opposite choice feel irresponsible: leaving a threshold un-crossed is leaving money on the table, failing to use a coupon is throwing it away, and the real purchase recedes behind the game of optimising the deal. The mathematics becomes a kind of entertainment, which is precisely the point. A shopper who is enjoying the game of discounts is not asking whether the item was needed in the first place, and the question of need is the only one that actually determines whether money was saved.

There is a reasonable case for the festival, and it is worth stating plainly. For a large part of the population, the November sales are the one moment in the year when durable goods, the phone, the laptop, the winter coat, can be bought at a price that would otherwise be out of reach. The discounts are a genuine redistribution of purchasing power toward the price-sensitive, and the elaborate game is the price mechanism's way of funding that redistribution with attention instead of margin. None of this is contemptible.

What is worth noticing is how the arithmetic colonises the rest of the year. The festival trains the habit of waiting for the discount, of treating the list price as an opening position in a negotiation, of measuring value by the gap between the sticker and the sale. A price becomes a thing to beat rather than a thing to pay, and every ordinary purchase, for a book or a bus ticket or a cup of coffee, inherits a small shadow of the November game. The festival itself is harmless, and mostly enjoyable. The habit it trains, that nothing should be bought at its face value, is the part that lasts, and it is a more expensive habit than any of the discounts will ever repay.
