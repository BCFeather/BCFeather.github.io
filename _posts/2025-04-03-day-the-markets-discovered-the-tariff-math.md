The day after the administration announced its broad tariff programme, the world's markets delivered the arithmetic that the policy had created, and the arithmetic was the largest single-day sell-off in years, the trillions of dollars in value erased across the world's exchanges, and the trading desks working through the night to model the new economics. The announcement, delivered on the day that the administration had named Liberation Day, had been anticipated for weeks; the reaction had not, and the gap between the anticipation and the reaction was the day's real lesson. The markets had priced the possibility of the tariffs; they had not priced the version that arrived.

The announcement itself was the part that the markets had struggled to model, because the design was the maximum version. The programme applied the levies broadly, to the goods from virtually every trading partner, at the rates that the administration had calculated as the reciprocal of the barriers the partners impose; the rates varied by country, with the largest applied to the partners with the largest surpluses, and the effect, the economists calculated, was the largest increase in the effective tariff burden in nearly a century. The markets had been prepared for the tariffs in some form; the breadth and the rates exceeded the prepared-for version, and the excess was the gap that the sell-off closed.

The reaction in the equity markets was the fastest and the most visible part of the day. The indexes fell through the session with the particular cascade that such announcements produce, the sell programmes triggering the sell programmes, the volatility measures spiking to the levels that the market had not seen since the crises of the past decade, and the closing levels, published in the red, carrying the losses that the analysts would spend the night explaining. The losses were concentrated in the sectors with the international exposure, the technology, the consumer goods, the industrials that run the global supply chains, and the concentration was the market's way of saying that the tariffs' burden would be carried, at least initially, by the companies that the tariffs were designed to protect.

The bond market's reaction was the quieter and more consequential part of the day. The yields moved with the flight to safety, the shorter maturities rallying as the investors sought the shelter, and the longer maturities moving with the inflation expectations that the tariffs had raised; the central bank's next moves, the futures now showed, were being repriced around the inflation that the policy would bring, and the repricing was the market's admission that the tariffs had changed the economic outlook that the year had been built on. The bond market's message was the day's deepest one: the policy that the markets had been treating as a negotiating instrument had arrived as an economic event.

The aftermath of the day will be conducted in the two registers that the markets have developed for such moments: the technical and the political. The technical work, the models, the hedges, the repositioning, will run through the nights ahead, as the desks price the sectors, the countries and the companies that the new tariff map redraws; the political work, the negotiations, the exemptions, the carve-outs, will run in the capitals, as the partners and the domestic industries press their cases. The day after the announcement established the costs that the policy imposes; the weeks after will establish the politics that will decide whether the costs are paid, shared or reversed. The markets discovered the tariff math on 3 April, and the discovery will be the reference point for the year's trade politics, whichever way the negotiations go.
