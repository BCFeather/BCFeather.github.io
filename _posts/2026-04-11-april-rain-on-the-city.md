The rain that falls in April is a different kind of weather. It does not arrive the way a summer storm does, with thunder and a threat. It begins almost without notice, a thin steady veil that the sky lowers over the city and then keeps there, for an hour or an afternoon or, sometimes, the better part of a day. People open umbrellas reluctantly, as if admitting that it has started, and the streets take on a quieter, slower tone.

The first thing it does is change the colour of the world. Pavements go dark and glossy, the greys of concrete turning almost black, and the new leaves on the plane trees, which had been a dusty pale green, deepen to something rich and wet. The asphalt holds the light differently; headlights stretch across it in long reflections, and the neon of shopfronts runs in rivulets along the kerb. A city that has been matte and dust-grey for a long winter suddenly looks washed, as if someone had turned the saturation up.

The second thing it does is slow the people. On a dry April day the sidewalks are crowded with people walking fast, jackets open, already half in a hurry toward the summer that has not yet arrived. In the rain the pace drops. Umbrellas negotiate past one another with a little more care. Conversations, shielded under awnings, run longer than they meant to. The rain gives everyone a small, shared excuse to pause, and the city takes it. Nobody is quite sure whether to be pleased or inconvenienced, and the uncertainty itself is pleasant.

There is a particular sound to this rain. It is not the drumming of a hard summer downpour on a tin roof. It is a low, even hush, the kind that muffles the usual city noise, the bus engines and the shouted names, and leaves only the tick of droplets off awnings and the soft swish of tyres on wet road. Inside a café, behind a steamed window, the noise of the street recedes to a murmur, and people read or talk more quietly than they would. The rain, without asking, lowers the whole city's voice.

What is most noticeable is what it does not do. It does not flood, it does not threaten, and it does not drive people inside out of fear. It is a gentle, unhurried water, the kind that the farmers wait for and the gardeners welcome, and the city, urban and hurried as it is, still recognizes it as good weather. Children splash through a puddle with the same unembarrassed joy they show in July, and old people stand at the doorways looking at the sky as if checking on an old acquaintance.

By late afternoon the sky lightens, the veil lifts, and the pavements steam a little in the weak sun. The umbrellas fold away. The city speeds up again, the way it does, and the rush resumes as if it had never paused. But for the hours the rain lasted, the whole place moved at a lower gear, and for a while, that was enough.
