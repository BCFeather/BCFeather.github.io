A section of the George VI ice shelf in the Antarctic Peninsula collapsed in early August, releasing an iceberg roughly the size of a small country into the sea that separates the peninsula from the continent, and the event had been forecast to the month by the models that track the region's warming. The collapse was announced by the satellite teams with the matter-of-fact tone that such announcements have acquired, and the coverage, dutiful and brief, moved on by the next news cycle. The predictability is the disturbing part: the shelf failed exactly where and when the science said it would, and the failure of the forecasters to be surprised is the loudest detail in the whole event.

The science of the collapse is a story of thin ice and warm water. The George VI shelf floats in a channel where the currents bring the deep warm water against its underside, and the shelf has been thinning from below for two decades while the summer meltwater has been weakening it from above. The models that track the two processes have been publishing, with increasing confidence, the dates by which particular sections would lose their connection to the pinning points that hold them in place, and the August collapse removed one of the largest remaining sections on the forecast list. The event is not a surprise to the discipline; it is a scheduled payment on a debt that the region has been accumulating.

The geography of the collapse matters beyond the peninsula. The ice shelves do not themselves raise the sea level, because they float, but they act as the brakes on the glaciers behind them, and when a shelf breaks, the glaciers accelerate toward the sea with nothing holding them back. The glaciers feeding the George VI channel have been among the fastest-moving on the continent, and the August collapse removed the restraint that kept the acceleration in check. The full consequence will take years to measure, and the measurements will arrive through the same satellites that watched the collapse, which is the quiet reassurance of the story: the region is being watched with an instrument density that the previous generation of glaciologists could only dream of.

The Antarctic response to the event has been the usual and unsatisfactory one, a mixture of formal statements about the continent's treaty system and a budget process that continues to treat the region's monitoring as an optional expense. The scientists who have been tracking the peninsula's warming for three decades have learned to translate their findings into the language of insurance and shipping and geopolitical interest, because the language of science alone has not been enough to fund the continuous observation that the region now clearly requires. The shelf collapsed where it was being watched; the parts of the coast that are not being watched are the ones that will fail without warning, and the number of those parts is larger than the monitoring budget admits.

The deeper lesson of the August collapse is that the region's future is no longer a matter of scientific uncertainty, which was the comfortable version of the story, but of timing and consequence, which is the uncomfortable one. The models that forecast this collapse have been wrong in their details and right in their direction, and the direction has now been confirmed by the event itself. The sea will keep rising, the shelves will keep failing, and the calendar of the failures will keep being written by the same combination of warm water and thin ice that the scientists have been describing for years. The collapse that was forecast to the month is the proof that the discipline understands the system; the question the discipline cannot answer, because it belongs to politics rather than to science, is whether the understanding will arrive in time to change the schedule.
