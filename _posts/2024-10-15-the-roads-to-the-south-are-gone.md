---

At noon on October 15, the northern sections of the roads that once connected the two Koreas were blown up, and the explosion was watched, from the southern side of the border, by the soldiers who had been told to expect it. North Korea's military demolished the Gyeongui road, the western link to Seoul that had been a symbol of the inter-Korean detente of the past decades, and the Donghae road on the eastern coast, along with the railway lines that ran beside them; the South Korean military, which had detected the preparations, fired warning shots across the border in response, and the two sides settled into the new reality that the demolition had declared. The General Staff in Pyongyang described the action as part of the phased and complete separation of its territory from the South, a phrase that had been the diplomatic euphemism of the year for what the leadership was preparing to do.

The roads were not, in any practical sense, in use. The border has been closed to ordinary travel since the pandemic, the railways have carried nothing but symbolism for years, and the demolition removed infrastructure that had already ceased to function; the significance of the act was therefore entirely political, and it was read as such in Seoul. The year has been one of deliberate rupture on the Korean Peninsula: the North's leadership has called the South a 'hostile state' and a 'primary enemy', the constitution has been amended to write the enmity into law, and the drones that crossed the border in recent weeks, whatever their origin, were used as the pretext for the final severing of the ties that remained. The demolition was the punctuation mark on a policy that has spent the year dismantling, piece by piece, the framework of engagement that the previous decades had built.

The timing carried its own message. The demolition came at the end of a year in which the South's government had responded to the North's provocations with its own hardening line, the alliance with the United States strengthened, the joint exercises expanded, and the language from Seoul about the North's regime growing more confrontational in turn. The two Koreas have spent the year speaking past each other in the vocabulary of escalation, and the roads, which had survived the wars of the past, were the casualty of a confrontation that neither leadership appears willing to walk back.

The peninsula's dynamics have shifted beneath the surface. The North's turn inward, toward its own military and its alliance with Russia, has made the inter-Korean relationship increasingly peripheral to its strategy; the South, for its part, has begun to plan for a future in which reunification is no longer assumed, and the demolition of the roads, once unthinkable, was absorbed by the South Korean public with remarkable calm. The explosion was small, the roads were symbolic, and the escalation was, in military terms, minimal.

And yet the act deserves more attention than its material weight suggests. The roads were the last physical infrastructure of the engagement era, and their destruction formalised what the year had already established: the two Koreas no longer treat each other as parties to a conversation, but as enemies to be managed. The warning shots that answered the demolition were the smallest possible demonstration of that new understanding, and the months ahead, as the North continues its drift and the South adjusts to it, will show what the roads' disappearance means.
