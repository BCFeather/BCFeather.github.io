---

On 9 March a small molecule designed entirely by a generative model entered phase I trials in Oxford, and the announcement carried the weight of a promise finally kept. For the better part of a decade the phrase 'AI-designed drug' has been used loosely, usually to describe a database search with a fancy front end. This candidate is different in one respect that matters: the starting structure itself came from a model that had been trained on protein-ligand data, and no human chemist proposed the scaffold that the team then synthesized and tested. The molecule was invented, in the ordinary sense of the word, by software.

Phase I is a milestone and a trap at the same time. It proves that the compound is safe enough to give to a small number of healthy volunteers, and it says almost nothing about whether the drug will work. Most candidates that enter phase I never make it to a pharmacy, and the attrition rate is not kind to new modalities. The honest reading of this week is that the industry has finally reached the stage where AI-designed molecules fail or succeed on the same terms as everyone else's, which is more progress than it sounds like, because for years they were judged on the terms of the announcement alone.

I have been sceptical of this field since the days when every lab claimed to have 'solved' drug discovery by re-arranging public databases, and I remain wary of the hype cycle that follows any first. But I also think the scepticism needs updating. What has changed quietly is not the creativity of the models but the quality of the data going in, and the discipline of the validation coming out. The Oxford team published its synthesis and its animal data before asking anyone to believe in the molecule, which is the exact opposite of the usual press-release-first approach, and that alone distinguishes it from a decade of predecessors.

The bigger question is what happens downstream. A molecule entering the clinic is not a business model. Manufacturing, toxicology, phase II design and the slow grind of patient recruitment still look exactly like they did before the models arrived, and no amount of compute compresses a two-year follow-up into a quarter. The value that generative chemistry adds, if it adds any, is at the front of the pipeline, where it can widen the funnel of ideas that reach the clinic at all. That is a real contribution, but it is a modest one, and the industry's habit of pricing it as if it were the cure for everything is the main reason I hesitate to celebrate.

My own conclusion, for what it is worth, is that this is the most ordinary and therefore most encouraging moment in the history of AI drug discovery. The molecule is not a miracle; it is an experiment like any other, with a real chance of failing and a real chance of teaching us something when it does. The field stops being science fiction the day its products are allowed to fail in public, and that day, in Oxford, has now arrived. What the model contributed can be measured, which means it can also be questioned, and that is exactly the condition under which medicine makes progress.
