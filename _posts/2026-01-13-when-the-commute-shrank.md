title: "When the commute shrank"

description: "Five years after remote work became mainstream, the hour-long journey to the office has become a minority habit, and the cities that grew around it are still adjusting. A short essay on what a city loses and gains when the daily migration stops."
---

There was a time, not long ago, when the shape of a city was written in its commute. The suburbs existed because the centre did, the trains ran on a timetable drawn from the working day, and the rush hour was the city's pulse, a daily migration of millions moving in and out of the same narrow streets at the same two moments. Then remote work became ordinary, and within a few years the migration shrank. The train timetables still exist, but they run emptier; the office districts still stand, but they have become places people visit rather than inhabit. The city is adjusting to a commuter who no longer commutes, and the adjustment is revealing things about both.

The gains of the vanished commute are real and easily listed. Time, first of all: the hours once spent in transit have been returned to their owners, and a generation has quietly reclaimed something like two months of waking life per year. The geography of opportunity has flattened as well. A person whose job is remote can live where housing is honest, where family is, where the air is better, and the old equation that tied livelihood to a specific zip code has loosened for a large slice of the workforce. For those people the shrunken commute is not a loss but a liberation, and the nostalgia for the old commute is mostly a nostalgia held by people who did not have to do it.

The costs are slower to show and easier to miss. A city is more than its offices, but its offices were engines for everything else: the sandwich shops, the cleaners, the security staff, the whole service economy that existed because tens of thousands of people needed lunch and coffee in the same hour. When the commuters stayed home, those jobs did not disappear, but they moved into the suburbs and onto delivery bikes, and the people who perform them now work longer routes for the same money. The casual encounter, the colleague from another floor, the conversation at the water cooler that solved a problem nobody had written down, all of it thinned out with the traffic, and the informal knowledge of an organisation, which no system can capture, thinned out with it.

The deepest change is a quiet division between two kinds of work. Some jobs went remote because they could; others remained bound to place because the work itself is physical, caring, custodial or clinical. The same shift that gave one class of worker control over where they live has made the other class more necessary in more places, and the two now inhabit different relationships to the city. One negotiates with it; the other serves it in person. The commute, in this sense, never disappeared; it was redistributed, and the people still making it every day are making it for everyone else's convenience.

The useful question is not whether remote work was good or bad, because it was plainly both, and the balance varies from person to person. It is whether the city, the timetable and the wage structure will finish adapting to the new distribution, or remain built for a migration that no longer happens. Cities have absorbed bigger transformations before, and they will absorb this one, but the interval between the change and the adjustment is where the discomfort lives. The commute shrank in a year; the city that was built around it will take longer.
