In the last fortnight before the exam, the school changes character. The regular classes thin out, the bell schedule loosens, and the centre of gravity of the whole building migrates to the self-study room, where the rows of desks fill earlier each morning and empty later each evening. Nobody lectures. Nobody asks questions out loud. The work has become, in these final weeks, almost entirely private and almost entirely collective at the same time, and the room hums with the particular quiet of a great many people doing the same thing alone.

The physical atmosphere of the place is its own kind of climate. The lights are on even in the daytime, because the blinds are drawn against the May sun and against the rest of the world. The desks are arranged in long, neat files, each one carrying its owner's small domestic siege equipment: a water bottle, a stack of past papers, a pack of pens, a timetable pinned into a plastic sleeve, a photograph or a sticky note that no one else reads. Over the weeks these desks have become territories, and returning to the same one each day is part of how the discipline holds itself together.

What is striking is how little of the effort is actually about learning new material. In the countdown weeks, almost no one is encountering a concept for the first time. The work is maintenance and rehearsal and triage: redoing the problems one keeps getting wrong, reading over the notes one made months ago, drilling the formulas that still slip. It is less like studying than like tuning an instrument already built, checking every string before a performance one cannot opt out of. The pace is steady rather than frantic, because by now the frantic phase has already happened and is understood to have been, mostly, wasted energy.

There is a strange camaraderie that is also deliberately not camaraderie. The people in the room are, officially, competitors for the same limited places, and yet they share the room without ever speaking about that. They do not compare scores out loud. They pass a spare eraser across an aisle. They leave at the same hour, walk out into the same warm evening, and do not congratulate or commiserate, because the result is two weeks away and there is nothing to say yet. The solidarity is the solidarity of shared endurance, and it is one of the strongest things about the experience, even as it is never named.

This is the part of the process that the exam itself, when it finally arrives, cannot reproduce. The self-study room is a space built entirely around waiting: waiting for a date that is written on the board at the front and ticks down by one each morning, waiting to be assessed on three years of work, waiting for the door to finally open onto whatever comes next. The waiting is the substance. When it ends, on the second week of June, the room will empty overnight, and the desks that were territories for months will be anonymous again within days.

For the people still sitting there now, the countdown has its own grim arithmetic, and the fluorescent light is the same at nine at night as it was at seven in the morning. The last two weeks are not about getting smarter. They are about arriving at the exam having done, one more time, everything that has already been done, and trusting that the repetition was enough.
