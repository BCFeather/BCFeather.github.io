On 5 June, the World Health Organization and the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention stood together in a briefing room and launched a joint continental preparedness and response plan for the Ebola outbreak spreading through the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo and into Uganda. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, one of the milder but still dangerous strains, and it was declared a public health emergency of international concern on 17 May. The new plan runs from June through November and is asking for 518 million dollars to support African states in watching for, detecting and containing cases before they travel.

The shape of the plan is what matters. This is not an appeal to fly in foreign teams at the first alarm. It is a deliberate bet on African capacity: a risk-based, community-centred framework aligned with the continent's own health-security agenda, meant to keep surveillance, laboratories, case management and community outreach running without interruption across neighbouring countries. The logic is that Ebola moves slowly enough, and across enough borders, that a region-wide surveillance net can outrun it if the net is in place before cases arrive. The cost of being ready is a fraction of the cost of the last response, which is always the argument and rarely the budget.

The context gives the gamble its urgency. The outbreak is centred on a part of the continent where movement across borders is constant and where health systems, after years of strain, are uneven. Bundibugyo is less lethal than the Zaire strain that tore through West Africa a decade ago, which is precisely why it is easy to under-react: it spreads quietly, looks like other fevers early on, and can gain a hold before anyone notices. Two confirmed cases have already been exported to Uganda, which is the warning sign the plan is built around.

The funding ask is the hard part. Half a billion dollars over six months is not a vast sum in the architecture of global health, but it has to arrive quickly and it has to arrive before the caseload does. Past experience is that money flows generously once bodies are in the headlines and reluctantly when the cases are still a trickle. The plan's architects are, in effect, asking donors to pay for a summer in which nothing dramatic happens, which is the least satisfying kind of philanthropy.

The politics of the moment cut both ways. On one hand, the international travel season is building, with large gatherings and millions of people on the move, which raises the stakes of any missed case. On the other, the world's attention is fragmented by conflicts and economic worries, and Ebola in central Africa does not yet have the grip on the public imagination that a visible outbreak elsewhere would command.

What the launch establishes is that the response is being run at a continental scale rather than a country-by-country scramble. Whether that translates into funded surveillance stations, trained local contact-tracers and laboratories that can turn a sample around in a day is the test of the coming weeks. The plan itself is the easy half. The hard half is the money, and the patience to act before the story becomes urgent.
