On 13 October, the CRAFT facility, the comprehensive research facility for fusion reactor key systems known by the name of the ancient giant Kua Fu, reached a milestone of the unglamorous kind that fusion progress is made of: the prototype of its divertor passed expert review and acceptance. The divertor is not a name that appears in the popular accounts of fusion; it is, however, one of the hardest components in the entire enterprise, the piece of the machine that must absorb the exhaust of the fusion reaction, the heat and particles that pour out of the plasma, and survive. The prototype's acceptance, with a steady-state heat load capacity of twenty megawatts per square metre and a plasma-facing surface accurate to under a millimetre, is a step toward the reactor core that fusion research has been building toward for decades.

The numbers deserve a moment of attention. Twenty megawatts per square metre is a heat load that would destroy almost any ordinary material; the divertor must face it continuously, in a vacuum, while holding its shape precisely enough for the plasma to remain stable. The millimetre-level accuracy of the surface, the report noted, is the tolerance that the machine demands, and the combination of the two, the ability to absorb extremes and to hold precision under them, is what makes the component one of the field's hardest engineering problems.

The significance of the acceptance is best understood in the context of the broader fusion effort. A working fusion power plant will require not only a plasma that produces net energy but a set of components that can survive the conditions the plasma creates, and the divertor is the leading edge of that problem. The CRAFT facility exists to test exactly these systems, the ones that no laboratory plasma has yet faced at power-plant scale, and each accepted prototype, each component that passes review, moves the enterprise from the physics of fusion toward the engineering of it.

The pace of the work is its own signal. The review came after years of design and construction, and the facility's steady progress through the hardest components, the shielding, the materials, the structures that will face the plasma, is the patient, expensive half of fusion that the headlines rarely mention. The achievements are measured in tolerances and megawatts rather than in ignition records, and they are as necessary as any of the celebrated milestones. The work also exemplifies the field's peculiar discipline: the knowledge that every part of a fusion machine must be proven separately, before the whole can be assembled, so that the programme's calendar is filled not with discoveries but with reviews, acceptances and the slow accumulation of approved components.

The prototype's acceptance does not mean a fusion reactor is near; the distance from a component to a power plant remains long, and every honest account of the field says so. But it means the component exists, tested and approved, and the machine that will need it is one step closer to being buildable. The hardest piece of the fusion core has been made, and measured, and accepted, and the long road to fusion power, built one impossible component at a time, has advanced by the width of a shield.
