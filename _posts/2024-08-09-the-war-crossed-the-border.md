On the morning of 6 August, the war crossed the border, and it did so not in the direction everyone had spent two years watching. Ukrainian units, reported by Russian officials at around a thousand men with tanks and armoured vehicles, pushed across the frontier into the Russian region of Kursk, through the town of Sudzha and toward the settlements that line the road to the regional capital, and Moscow's response, from the first statements of the border guards to Vladimir Putin's address the next day, was the language of shock dressed up as routine. The president called the incursion a 'large-scale provocation', which was accurate in scale, and the defence ministry called it a border incident, which was accurate in nothing.

The significance is hard to overstate, and easy to misstate. No foreign army had entered Russian territory in force since the Second World War, and the border that the Kremlin had treated as a guarantee of domestic calm was suddenly a front line, with evacuated villages, columns of civilians and the first air strikes falling on Russian soil. The war, which had been fought almost entirely on Ukrainian territory since February 2022, had acquired a second geography overnight, and the psychological effect on a Russian public that had been told, in effect, that the war was something happening elsewhere, far away and sanitised, was probably worth as much to Kyiv as the ground itself.

What Ukraine hopes to gain is a matter of debate, and the options on the table are the usual ones in such operations. A diversion, to pull Russian reserves away from the grinding battles in the Donbas; a bargaining chip, to be traded for Ukrainian territory in whatever negotiations eventually begin; a demonstration, to show the partners supplying weapons that Ukrainian forces can still seize the initiative and that their aid buys results; and, not least, a wound that Moscow cannot easily close without admitting how serious the breach is. The motives are probably mixed, and they do not need to be pure to be effective.

The risks are as real as the gains. Armoured columns that cross a border can be cut off behind it, the supply lines lengthen and the reserves that Russia has not yet committed are still large, and the Russian response, once it organises, will not be gentle, for the same reason that the incursion is significant: a state that has built its domestic legitimacy on the inviolability of its borders cannot afford to leave an invasion unanswered. The next weeks will test whether the operation is a raid with a deadline or a front with a future, and the answer will depend less on the ground seized than on the fighting around it.

For all the uncertainty, the crossing has already changed something durable. The taboo is broken, the border is no longer a line that only one side may use, and every calculation in Moscow about 'the special operation' now has to include the possibility that the war comes home. Wars have a way of doing that; the surprise is only that it took this one so long, and that it happened at a place nobody had predicted. The map that the commentators had grown comfortable with, a fixed front in the east and a frozen sky above it, is obsolete, and the war, whatever its eventual shape, will not fit back into it.
