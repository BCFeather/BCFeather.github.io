Parkinson's disease has a founding story that most people can recite: the loss of dopamine-producing cells in a deep brain region, and the motor symptoms, tremor, stiffness, slowness, that follow. The story is true as far as it goes, and it has guided treatment for decades, from levodopa to deep brain stimulation. A paper published in *Nature* in early February argues that the story is incomplete, and that the disease is better understood as a disorder of a network that spans the body and the mind, a somato-cognitive action network, rather than as the failure of a single chemical system.

The team, led by researchers at the Changping Laboratory in Beijing, analysed the disease at the level of functional networks rather than individual regions. The distinction is more than conceptual. A region-based view asks which cells die and what chemical they make; a network view asks how circuits coordinate, and where the coordination breaks down. The authors identify a network connecting motor control with cognitive and bodily regulation, and they report that this network, not a single nucleus, is where the pathology concentrates. Parkinson's, in this reading, is not one thing failing but a system coming apart, and the motor symptoms are the visible part of a larger disruption.

The striking part of the report is the intervention. The team did not stop at describing the network; they targeted it directly, and in clinical study the intervention produced improvement. The details of the intervention, its scale and its durability, will matter enormously, and the paper's own framing is appropriately careful about what a first report can claim. But the direction is significant: if the network can be modified therapeutically, then the disease is no longer only about replacing a missing chemical but about rebalancing a system, a much broader set of possibilities.

The finding also explains, in retrospect, why Parkinson's has always been more than a motor disease. The same patients who tremor and stiffen also struggle with sleep, mood, autonomic function and cognition, features that the dopamine story never quite accounted for. A network that integrates motor and cognitive control makes these symptoms cohere: they are not separate complications but different expressions of one disrupted circuit. The disease's famous heterogeneity, the way it looks different in every patient, is what a network account would predict, and that is a sign that the model is pointing at something real.

The paper will not end the debate, and the dopamine story will not be discarded; levodopa works, and no network theory should pretend otherwise. What the work does is enlarge the frame, and it does so at a moment when network views are reshaping how many brain diseases are conceived. If Parkinson's is a network disorder, then treatments aimed at restoring network function, and not merely at topping up a chemical, deserve a serious place in the pipeline. Sixty years after dopamine made the disease the most treatable of the major neurodegenerative conditions, a new generation of research is asking whether the treatment has been aimed at the right target all along.
