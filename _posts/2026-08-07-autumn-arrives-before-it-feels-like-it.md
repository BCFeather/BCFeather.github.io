There is a point in the year when the calendar insists the summer is over and the air disagrees. The heat does not relent for weeks after, the cicadas keep their long note, and the afternoon sun falls on the street with the same white force it has held since July. And yet, somewhere in the body, a door has opened. It is not the weather that has changed; it is the idea of the weather, and the idea arrives first.

The old calendar of the season understood this patience. It names the turning before the world has turned, and the naming itself becomes a kind of preparation. People note the date the way they note the first swallow, not because the evidence is conclusive but because the waiting is part of the thing. A season is not only a temperature. It is a story the city tells itself, and the story starts being told a little ahead of the facts.

In these days that are still hot but no longer feel like mid-July, small adjustments begin. The light goes earlier by a few minutes, and the evening walk, which at the height of summer could not start before half past seven, becomes possible a touch sooner. The sky at dusk keeps its colour a little longer, and the shadows sharpen. None of it would register on a thermometer. All of it registers on the people who live inside the year.

There is a particular quietness to this waiting season. The peak of summer is loud: the midday glare, the shutters, the air-conditioners humming against the walls. The turn that has not yet arrived is quieter. The city is still running its summer programme, the stalls still sell cold noodles, the fountains still run, but at the edges the rhythm loosens. A student browsing a bookshop finds the new term on their mind unbidden. An adult walking home notices that the days have begun their slow shortening and feels, without putting it into words, that the year is leaning into its second half.

This gap between the calendar and the weather is worth sitting with. Modern life tends to demand that seasons be experienced immediately, that a heatwave be called a heatwave on the day it arrives and forgotten the day it passes. But the older way of noticing is slower. It holds that autumn begins not when the air cools but when the mind is ready to notice the cooling, and that the readiness arrives a fortnight ahead. The season, on this view, is as much an act of attention as a fact of the sky.

The heat will return, of course. The turning of a solar term is not the turning of the year. There are weeks of warm afternoons ahead, and the air over the asphalt will hold its glare well into September. Nobody who lives through it mistakes the season's promise for its arrival. But to mark the date is to keep a small appointment with one's own sense of time, and these appointments are how a year is felt as more than a succession of days.

The city will go on sweating through August. That is not in doubt. What has changed, quietly, is that the waiting for the other season has begun. The air has not turned. The mind has.
