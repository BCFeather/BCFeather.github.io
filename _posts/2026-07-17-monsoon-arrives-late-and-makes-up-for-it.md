India's monsoon declared itself on 12 July, more than a week after the date that the meteorological office had forecast and two weeks after the farmers who trusted the forecast had planted for it, and the rain that followed has been heavy enough to erase the deficit and, in the northern districts, to threaten the very fields it was meant to save. The monsoon is the world's most important weather system, the annual migration of moisture that feeds the harvest of a fifth of humanity, and it has been running late, running strong and running its own timetable, and the mixture is the kind of forecast problem that keeps the region's climatologists humble.

The delay was the visible part of the story. The monsoon's onset is the event that the subcontinent plans around, the sowing dates, the reservoir releases, the insurance deadlines all set to the climatological average, and a late onset is the kind of news that moves the markets before it moves the fields. The meteorological office's forecast, issued a month out, predicted a normal onset, and the actual arrival missed the forecast by more than a week, which is the difference between a good forecast and a useful one. The post-mortems will be careful to note that the error was within the historical range, and they will be right, and the farmers who planted early because the forecast said so will be less comforted by the range than by the rain.

The rain that followed has been the more complicated part of the season. The late monsoon arrived with a burst that the meteorologists describe as a well-organized system, and the first fortnight has delivered above-normal totals across most of the country, wiping out the deficit that the delay had created and pushing the northern districts toward the other problem, the waterlogging that damages the young crops it was meant to water. The irony is the season's signature: a monsoon that was late and is now heavy, with the reservoirs refilling and the district authorities issuing drainage warnings in the same week that the sowing data was being revised upward.

The institutional response to the season has been the quietly impressive part. The reservoir coordination, the release schedules that balance the upstream storage against the downstream flood risk, has been run this year with a granularity that the previous decades never achieved, because the telemetry that the river basins now carry has turned the monsoon's management from an art into a schedule. The insurance programmes that the last crisis produced have paid out faster than before, the seed banks have released the flood-tolerant varieties that the trials have validated, and the farmers' helplines have been answering in the languages that the fields speak. The systems are catching up to the weather, and the catching up is the part of the story that the coverage of the delay keeps missing.

The late, heavy monsoon of 2026 is the season that the resilience programmes were built for, and the verdict on them will be measured in the harvest rather than in the rainfall. The deficit-recovery arithmetic says the season is back on track; the waterlogging reports say the track is bumpier than the totals suggest; and the climate models say that the variability that produced this season, the delayed onset, the intense bursts, the uneven distribution, is the pattern that the warming atmosphere is making more common. The monsoon will keep running its own timetable, and the system that learns to plant for the variability rather than for the average will be the system that the farmers trust. The season's first month has been a lesson in both, delivered in the form of rain.
