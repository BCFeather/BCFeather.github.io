A comet discovered in January by a survey telescope in Hawaii is making its first and only close approach this month, and the object has brightened into the kind of visitor that the evening sky offers once a decade: visible to the naked eye from the northern hemisphere, with a tail that the photographers are already calling the best of the century so far. The amateur networks that track such visitors have organized themselves with the speed that their kind has perfected, the finder charts, the imaging schedules, the coordinated observations that turn a sky full of enthusiasts into a global instrument, and this month is their reward. The comet is a reminder that the sky's calendar is written by bodies that owe us nothing.

The science of the visitor is more interesting than the spectacle, and the two are connected. The comet is a long-period object, a fragment of the solar system's deep freeze that has spent most of its existence in the cloud of debris beyond the planets, and its orbit, which will not bring it back for a hundred thousand years, carries the chemical fingerprint of the region where the planets formed. The spectroscopic campaigns this month are trying to read that fingerprint before the comet's surface is baked by its first close passage, and the readings will feed the models of how the solar system assembled itself. The bright tail that the photographers are celebrating is, for the spectroscopists, the contamination of the sample, the release of the volatile layer that the observations need to catch before it fades.

The public dimension of the event is the part that the astronomers value most, and the value is worth understanding. A naked-eye comet is one of the few astronomical events that pulls people away from their screens and out of their houses, and the surveys that follow the engagement numbers have learned to expect the spike: the observatory websites crashing, the planetarium tickets selling out, the school groups that suddenly want to know how comets work. The effect fades with the object, and the astronomers know it, and the durable part is the habit that a bright visitor can plant, the habit of looking up at a sky that the city lights have made invisible. The comet will not be back, and the habit, if the outreach teams do their job, will outlive the visitor.

The comet's arrival this month is a gift to a field that has been giving its attention to the sky's darker objects, the black holes, the exoplanets, the deep surveys that photograph the universe's childhood. The bright objects matter differently: they are the sky presenting itself, the reminder that the solar system is a working neighbourhood with a schedule of its own, and that the neighbourhood occasionally sends something beautiful past the door. The comet's passage is being observed by the amateurs with their backyard telescopes, by the professionals with their spectrographs, and by the public with their phones, and the three audiences are, for a few weeks, looking at the same object and seeing the same thing. That is a rare alignment, and the sky arranged it for free.
