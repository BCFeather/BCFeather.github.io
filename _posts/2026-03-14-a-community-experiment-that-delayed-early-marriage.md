Child marriage is one of those problems that statistics render abstract and lives make urgent. Globally it is measured in tens of millions of girls, its drivers are a tangle of poverty, education gaps, family economics and social norms, and its consequences, curtailed education, earlier childbearing, higher maternal and infant risk, are well documented. What is less common is rigorous evidence about what actually reduces it. A paper in the 12 March issue of *Nature* reports one of the clearer answers in years: a community-wide programme in northern Nigeria, aimed at girls' education and at the social norms surrounding marriage, reduced child marriage among the more than a thousand girls in the study.

The design matters because the intervention is not a handout. The programme worked at the level of the community, addressing the norms and expectations that sustain early marriage rather than only the individual girl or her family. Girls were supported to stay in and return to school, while the surrounding adults, parents, religious leaders, community elders, were engaged on the value of delayed marriage and girls' education. The premise, tested by the trial, is that a girl's marriage is decided not by her alone but by the web of people around her, and that changing the web changes the decision.

The results, the authors report, show a meaningful reduction in child marriage among the girls exposed to the programme, with the effect strongest where the intervention was most complete. The magnitude will be examined carefully, and the paper's own framing is appropriately measured: one region, one programme, a particular set of conditions. But the study is notable for what it tests, which is whether social-norm change can be delivered deliberately, and the answer, on this evidence, is that it can.

The finding sits inside a larger debate about how social change happens. There is a long tradition of assuming that legislation and policy shift practice from above, and laws against child marriage exist in many countries, with uneven effect. The Nigerian study is an argument from the other direction: that the binding constraint is often local, that the norms that keep early marriage alive are maintained at the community level, and that intervention at that level, however slow and unglamorous, can move the practice in ways that statutes alone have not. The authors are not claiming that law is irrelevant, only that it is insufficient without the social groundwork.

There is, finally, a moral measurement hidden in the study's design. A programme that treats families as partners rather than obstacles, that engages elders rather than bypasses them, assumes that the community is not the enemy of the girls it raises. The assumption is harder than it sounds, and the evidence suggests it is also useful: the community that sustains early marriage is the same community that can end it, if its norms are addressed rather than condemned. The paper is a careful, local, unglamorous study of a deeply entrenched practice, and it is exactly the kind of evidence that grand declarations about child marriage rarely provide.
