The Standing Committee of the National People's Congress closed its session on November 8 with the fiscal announcement that the markets had been waiting for all autumn. The committee approved a bill to raise the ceiling on local government special bonds by six trillion yuan, and the finance ministry, in the press conference that followed, laid out the full arithmetic: the six trillion, spread over three years, plus a further four trillion in special bond quotas issued over five years, make up roughly ten trillion yuan of resources for the purpose that the year has made urgent, the swapping of the hidden debts that local governments have accumulated outside the official ledger. The package is the largest single measure of the year's stimulus, and its announcement ended weeks of speculation about the size and the shape of the response.

The purpose of the swap is plain from the name. Local governments in China carry two kinds of debt: the official bonds that the markets see, and the hidden borrowings, through financing platforms and off-balance-sheet vehicles, that the accounting has kept out of view. The hidden debt has grown with the property downturn, as land sales, the traditional source of local revenue, have collapsed and the platforms have struggled to roll over their obligations; the swap converts the hidden liabilities into official, lower-cost bonds, giving the localities years of breathing room and the banks a cleaner balance sheet in exchange.

The size of the package is the part that the markets are still digesting. The ten trillion figure is large in absolute terms, and larger still as a signal, but the analysts have noted, and the finance ministry has conceded, that it is designed to manage the debt problem rather than to reflate the economy; the package contains no direct consumption stimulus, no large new infrastructure splurge, and the property market, the deepest source of the pressure, receives support mainly through the reduction of the burden around it. The message is stabilisation, and the stabilisation is the year's cautious answer to the question of how far Beijing will go.

The timing carries its own weight. The announcement came the same week as the American election, and the juxtaposition was not lost on anyone watching the two economies: Washington turning toward tariffs and confrontation, Beijing choosing the fiscal route of internal adjustment and managed growth. The package is also an answer to the year's own calendar, the property crisis, the deflationary drift, the weak consumption, all of it addressed through the mechanism that the state controls most directly, its own balance sheet.

The reaction in the markets was measured, which is the correct reaction to a package this carefully calibrated. The bond markets took the supply in stride, the currency held, and the equity markets moved on the news of the guarantee behind the localities rather than on any promise of a boom; the assessment, repeated across the analysts' notes, is that the swap improves the quality of the debt stock without changing the pace of the economy's rebalancing.

Beijing's ten trillion answer to its debt question is, at bottom, an act of consolidation. The hidden debts become visible, the visible debts become cheaper, and the localities receive the years that the transition needs; the package is the year's statement that the country will address its problems through the ledger rather than around it. The answer was long awaited, the answer was carefully sized, and the answer, for all its size, was an answer to the balance sheet, not to the growth rate.
