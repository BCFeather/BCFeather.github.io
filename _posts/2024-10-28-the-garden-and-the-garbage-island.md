Donald Trump held his largest rally of the campaign on October 27, filling Madison Square Garden in the city where he built his name, and the event was intended as the closing argument of a campaign that has narrowed to a handful of states. The speakers came and went, the arena was packed with supporters from across the country, and the candidate took the stage to deliver the familiar speech about the border, the economy and the America he promises to restore. The rally was also, as it turned out, the moment when the campaign's final week acquired its defining controversy: a comedian named Tony Hinchcliffe, one of the warm-up acts, told a joke about Puerto Rico, calling it 'a floating island of garbage in the middle of the ocean', and followed it with remarks about Latinos that leaned on the coarsest stereotypes. The joke was broadcast on the networks that carried the rally, and the backlash began before the candidate had left the stage.

The fallout moved fast, because the stakes were concentrated. Puerto Ricans do not vote in the presidential election as a state, but the diaspora votes, and the largest concentrations sit in the battlegrounds: Pennsylvania, with its Philadelphia suburbs, the state that the polls place at the centre of the map. The Harris campaign seized on the joke within the hour, the surrogates and the ads followed, and the Trump campaign, which had spent the week courting Latino voters with concerts and endorsements, was forced to distance itself from a remark it had not made. The candidate's advisers issued the statements, the campaign called the joke a bad one, and the candidate himself, whose rallies have always been the stage for such excesses, was left defending an event he had neither spoken nor planned.

The episode crystallised the choice of the campaign's closing weeks. The rally in the Garden was the latest and largest in a series of events that have leaned into grievance and insult, and the comedian's joke, whatever its author intended, fitted the pattern that the campaign's critics have described for months: the rhetoric of the stage, the cartoons of the opponents, the language that the supporters cheer and the swing voters recoil from. The defenders of the campaign saw the backlash as the predictable outrage of the other side, and the polls, which have moved little all autumn, will be the judges.

The storm over the joke has a deadline built into it. Nine days after the rally, the votes will be counted, and the controversy, which has occupied the final week, will either have moved the numbers or been absorbed by them. The history of such episodes in American elections is ambiguous: the outrage fades, the attention shifts, and the voters who were never going to change their minds are not changed. But the joke landed in the states where the election will be decided, on the eve of the early voting that has already begun, and the campaign's handling of it, the distance and the defiance, will be part of the final week's evidence.

The Garden was full, the candidate was on form, and the rally was the spectacle that the campaign had designed. The joke that escaped the stage, the island of garbage that now floats through the closing days of the campaign, was not in the script, and the script, which had held for months, is being rewritten by the fallout. The election will be decided, as the strategists say, by the voters who have not yet made up their minds, and the week that began in the Garden will end with the question of whether the garbage island moved any of them.
