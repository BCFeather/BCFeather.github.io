For years, India's ambition to build a domestic semiconductor industry has lived mostly in press releases and groundbreakings. On 16 May, the project took a small but concrete step toward becoming a real factory, when Tata Electronics and the Dutch lithography firm ASML signed an agreement to support a planned front-end chip fabrication plant in Dholera, Gujarat. The signing was witnessed in person by Narendra Modi and the prime minister of the Netherlands, a stage that underlines how seriously both governments take the deal.

A front-end fab is the part of the chip industry that actually manufactures silicon, as opposed to assembling or packaging it, and it is the segment India has conspicuously lacked. A country of more than a billion people, designing software and moving into electronics assembly, has until now bought nearly all of its advanced chips from abroad. The Dholera plant, billed as India's first front-end fab, is meant to begin closing that gap, and the ASML agreement is the kind of detail that turns that ambition from a slogan into an engineering project. ASML makes the extreme-ultraviolet and deep-ultraviolet lithography machines that are, in practice, the single indispensable tool for producing leading-edge silicon. No serious fab can exist without access to them, so tying the Dholera project to ASML is the difference between a plan and a factory.

The Netherlands' interest is as real as India's. ASML is the country's most strategically important company, a near-monopolist in the most advanced lithography, and the Dutch government spends a great deal of its diplomatic capital managing where that technology can and cannot go. Seeing its head of government on hand for the signing is a signal that the partnership is endorsed at the highest level, not merely a commercial contract between two firms. For the Netherlands, the arrangement opens a foothold in a market that European and Asian rivals have been courting for years.

What the announcement does not say is as important as what it does. It is an agreement and a memorandum, not a completed factory, and fabs of this kind take years and many billions of dollars to bring online. The timeline, the wafer size, the process node and the customer base were not the point of the ceremony, and they remain the questions that will decide whether Dholera is a landmark or another well-attended groundbreaking. Breaking ground and switching on production are separated by a long and unglamorous stretch of construction, permitting, equipment installation and yield ramp, and almost every country that has tried to enter the fab business has discovered that the hard part comes after the photographs.

Still, the direction of travel matters. The global semiconductor map, long centred on East Asia, has been quietly widening, with new fabs announced in the United States, in Europe and now, formally, in western India. Whether Dholera succeeds will depend on execution that has not yet begun. But on 16 May the project acquired the one partner no domestic fab can do without, and that is the moment at which an aspiration starts to cost real money.
