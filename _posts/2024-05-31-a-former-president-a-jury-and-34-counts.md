On 30 May, a jury of twelve New Yorkers found Donald Trump guilty on all 34 counts of falsifying business records, and American politics acquired a fact it had never contained before: a former president, and the presumptive nominee of a major party, convicted of a crime. The verdict came in the hush-money case, in which prosecutors argued that Trump had concealed a $130,000 payment made to the adult-film actress Stormy Daniels before the 2016 election, routing it through his lawyer Michael Cohen and then disguising the reimbursements as legal fees. The jury agreed, unanimously, that the records were falsified and that the falsification served an unlawful purpose.

The case itself was, by the standards of serious financial crime, a modest one. The charges were felonies only because the cover-up was alleged to have been designed to hide something bigger, a campaign-finance violation that never itself went to trial. No violence, no defrauded widows, no billion-dollar fraud. Yet the symbolic weight was enormous, and it is the symbolism that will define the moment in the history books: the office of the presidency, previously untouchable by criminal process, produced a defendant who sat through a trial in Manhattan and came out the other side with a conviction. The rule of law, in the narrowest and most literal sense, held.

The reactions were as familiar as they were divided. Trump called the trial rigged, promised an appeal, and insisted the case would be decided by the voters in November; his supporters treated the verdict as confirmation of a system weaponised against him; his critics described it as overdue accountability for a figure who had spent years treating the law as an obstacle rather than a constraint. Both readings contain some truth, which is exactly why the verdict is so hard to process. The trial was conducted with visible care by a judge determined to keep it ordinary, and the evidence, including Cohen's testimony and the paper trail of invoices and reimbursements, was concrete. It is also true that the case was brought by a district attorney whose political incentives were not invisible, and that the jury pool came from a county Trump lost heavily in 2016.

The immediate political consequences were oddly small. The conviction does not bar Trump from running, or from serving if he wins; the Republican Party, which had spent the spring consolidating behind him, showed no sign of wavering; and the polls, in the days after the verdict, moved only at the margins. The sentence, scheduled for 11 July, days before the party formally nominates him, is likely to be probation or a fine rather than prison, and an appeal will run well past the election. The verdict, in other words, was a thunderclap with a quiet echo: it changed the legal record of a man who may yet become president again, without changing, on its own, the arithmetic of the election.

And yet the longer view is different. A country that convicts a former president sends a message about itself, to itself, that is not easily retracted: that the highest office confers no immunity from its own courts. Whether that message strengthens the institutions that produced it, or embarrasses the half of the country that sees the prosecution as politics, will be decided by how the next six months unfold. For now, the fact stands on its own, the first conviction of a former president in American history, delivered by twelve people in a Manhattan courtroom, while the man at its centre prepared, with visible energy, to take the fight back to the voters.
