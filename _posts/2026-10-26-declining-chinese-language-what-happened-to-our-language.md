The Chinese language seems to be declining. On social media, conversations are filled with abbreviations, memes, and short emotional phrases. Complex sentences seem to be disappearing, and precise vocabulary is often replaced by a few popular expressions. Without some emojis, any request may seem extremely impolite. This anxiety is not meaningless. However, it may be more accurate to say that Chinese is not simply declining, but being reshaped by digital communication. What is truly at risk is not the language itself, but people's ability to think and express themselves in depth. From this perspective, therefore, what we should beware of is losing the ability to think clearly, rather than filing meaningless complaints about social media.

The anxiety itself is older than social media. Every generation since writing became widespread has believed that the language of the young is a falling-off from a golden standard. The complaint was levelled at telegraphs, at telephones, at text messages, at chat rooms, and it is being levelled again at short videos. There is a simple reason the worry never dies: the standard against which decline is measured is always the language of the previous generation, which was itself once accused of decay. Chinese has been changing for millennia, absorbing dialects, foreign borrowings and new characters, and it has never once actually died. That historical patience is worth keeping in mind, because it separates real loss from ordinary change.

Yet something in the current shift is genuinely different in degree. Writing is no longer mostly a private or deliberate act; it is public, immediate and permanent, performed in front of an audience that responds within seconds. Under those conditions, the optimal message becomes short, familiar and instantly legible, and so the incentive structure of everyday writing has quietly inverted. Where older forms of writing rewarded precision and a sustained argument, the feed rewards the phrase that is recognised fastest. People who write well in one context still write well in the other, but the volume of ordinary writing has moved so completely into the fast context that sustained prose is now something many people only read, never produce. The habit of thinking in full sentences, the kind that forces a thought to survive contact with grammar, is trained precisely by writing them, and that training has become rarer.

The most precise way to state the loss is not lexical but structural. The vocabulary of Chinese is not shrinking; new words appear faster than ever, and the expressive range of the language, considered as a whole, is wider than it was twenty years ago. What is thinning is the individual's exposure to long, careful prose, and with it the patience required to follow a complicated idea to its end. A language declines when the people who speak it stop demanding that it carry weight, when a summary is accepted in place of an argument and a catchphrase in place of a position. That demand is made by readers and writers together, and it is made privately, one sentence at a time, not by regulation or complaint.

None of this is an argument for purity, which never existed, or for nostalgia, which is useless. The internet has also enriched Chinese: it has given the language a faster, wittier vernacular, new genres of play, and a record of everyday speech that no previous century could preserve. The useful response is not to police usage but to keep the slow registers alive alongside the fast ones. Reading a book through to its last page, writing something longer than a paragraph without being paid to do so, refusing to let a meme stand in for a thought, these are quiet acts, but they are what the depth of a language actually consists of. Chinese is not declining, and it will not decline, unless the people who care about it stop doing the undramatic work of thinking in complete sentences.
