title: "Buses home for the holiday week"

description: "A short essay on the week when the big cities thin out and the small towns fill up, on the roads and stations and the quiet arithmetic of who leaves and who stays for the national holiday."
---

For a few days in early October, the map of the country does a slow reversal. The cities that filled up through the autumn term begin to empty, and the smaller towns and counties they emptied out to begin to fill. The trains run fuller than at any other point in the year, the long-distance coaches queue at the highway tolls, and a whole nation appears to be in transit at once. It is the holiday week, and it has a texture that no other week quite matches.

At the stations the crowd is of a particular kind. These are not travellers going somewhere new. They are people going somewhere they already know, carrying bags that hold laundry for the wash, gifts bought in the city, and the small obligations of a year lived apart. The queues move slowly because everyone is going the same direction at the same hour, and no one seems to mind very much. There is a patience about a homeward queue that a business queue lacks.

The journey itself is its own small ritual. You board in a city that is already a little lighter, the office floors half empty, the usual rush hour thinned by a third. You watch the towers give way to ring roads, then to fields and the last of the summer's stubble, then to the low hills that the place you are going to is tucked into. By the time the bus turns off the main road, the light has gone the soft gold it only gets this time of year, and the town looks as it always does, smaller in reality than it has been in memory.

What is striking is how little the destination has changed and how much you have. The street corner has the same stall, the same school wall, the same bridge over the same narrow river. But a few months of the city have rearranged the scale in your head, and the familiar now looks, quietly, smaller. That double feeling, of being both home and a visitor, is the whole point of these short returns. You are not staying. You are sampling the life you left, and then you will take the bus back.

The towns themselves behave differently during the week. The shops stay open late, the squares fill in the evening with people who have nowhere to be, and the usual quiet of a county seat is replaced by a temporary, loud sociability. Restaurants that are half empty on an ordinary Tuesday are booked out. The town has borrowed the city's crowds for seven days, and it spends them generously.

None of this is new. The holiday week has run on the same arithmetic for years: the young scattered to study and work, pulled back for a week by gravity that only the calendar can switch on. What changes is not the pattern but where you stand in it. At some point you stop being the one who is collected from the station and become, sometimes, the one waiting with the bags.

By the end of the week the flow reverses as quietly as it began. The buses fill again, the cities take their people back, and the small towns settle into their ordinary quiet, having spent their borrowed energy. The autumn continues. The week between being away and being home is over, and everyone has returned to the place they were heading to in the first place.
