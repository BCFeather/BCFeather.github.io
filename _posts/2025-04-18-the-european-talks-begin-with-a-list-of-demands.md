The trade negotiations between the two sides of the Atlantic opened in the third week of April, with the European negotiators carrying the mandate that the member states had authorised and the list of the goods and the sectors that the mandate was meant to protect. The opening positions were far apart: the American side insisted that the tariffs were the instrument of the renegotiation and would be lifted only for the concessions, and the European side insisted that the tariffs were unjustified and would be resisted until the normal trade was restored. The distance between the positions is the negotiation's first subject, and the talks' early sessions were spent, as such sessions always are, on the mapping of the distance rather than the crossing of it.

The European mandate, approved by the member states after the weeks of the conflict, is the part of the story that the negotiators describe as the strength of their position. The mandate authorises the removal of the tariffs on the American goods, subject to the terms of the negotiation; the mandate covers the sectors that the member states care most about, the automobiles, the machinery, the agriculture, and it carries the instruction that the agreement must preserve the European autonomy in the areas that the recent years have made sensitive. The mandate's construction reflects the politics of the member states, the balance between the exporters who want the deal and the industries that want the protection, and the balance is the negotiation's internal dimension, the one that the Europeans must manage alongside the external one.

The American position, as the talks opened, was the one that the year's announcements had established: the tariffs were the leverage, the leverage would be maintained until the terms were renegotiated, and the terms would be judged by the American arithmetic. The administration's negotiators carried the instructions that the doctrine provides, the product-by-product reciprocity, the attention to the trade balances, and the insistence that the agreements must show the results that the domestic politics can defend; the instructions leave less room for the flexibility that the European mandate assumes, and the mismatch is the negotiation's central difficulty. The two sides' frameworks are built on the different assumptions about what the talks are for, and the reconciliation of the assumptions is the work of the sessions.

The sectors and the industries that the talks affect have been following the proceedings with the attention that the stakes require. The European exporters, the automakers, the machinery makers, the food producers, have calculated the costs that the tariffs impose and the gains that the removal would bring, and the calculations have fed into the position papers that the negotiators carry; the American industries, the ones that the tariffs were meant to protect and the ones that the tariffs have hurt, have made their own calculations, and the calculations have reached the administration through the channels that the domestic politics provide. The industries' arithmetic is the background of the talks, and the arithmetic is the pressure that the negotiators feel from both directions.

The talks' outlook, after the opening sessions, is the outlook of the negotiations that have a long way to go: the positions are mapped, the calendar is set, and the work of the narrowing lies ahead. The deadlines that the pause provides, the ninety days that end in the summer, give the talks their schedule, and the schedule will determine whether the agreement can be reached within the window or whether the pause's end brings the escalation that the year's pattern threatens. The European talks began with a list of demands, and the list is the negotiation's starting point; the months ahead will show whether the list can become the basis of the agreement that both sides' politics can accept, and the showing will be the year's trade story in its most important chapter.
