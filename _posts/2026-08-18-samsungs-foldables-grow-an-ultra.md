---

Samsung's July Unpacked event produced the company's most complete foldable lineup to date: the Galaxy Z Fold8, the Flip8 and, new at the top, the Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra. Seven generations into the category, Samsung has stopped treating foldables as a niche and started treating them as a premium tier, and the Ultra model is the clearest statement of that shift. It is also, at 4.1 millimetres when unfolded and 215 grams, the thinnest and lightest device of its kind, which is how a product category signals it has matured.

The Ultra's pitch is productivity without compromise. Its eight-inch inner display is a genuine workspace for multitasking, its camera system jumps to 200 megapixels with HDR at full resolution, and its battery is sized for a full working day. The usual foldable trade-offs, the crease, the weight, the fragility, the camera that lagged a generation behind, are the things this device claims to have solved. Whether that claim survives daily use is what the first wave of reviews will test, and the reviews have been kinder to this generation than to any previous one, which is itself a signal.

The three-model split is the commercially interesting part. Samsung is now selling foldables the way it sells its flat phones: an Ultra for the maximalists, a standard model for the mainstream, and a Flip for the fashion end of the market. The strategy treats foldable phones as a full product family rather than a single experiment, and it is being matched across the industry, with rivals shipping their own folding devices on similar tiers. The foldable market has spent seven years proving it would not die; this generation is the first that looks like it is here to dominate the high end.

The AI angle runs through the whole launch. Samsung's pitch is that as AI becomes more agentic, the phone becomes the personal entry point to it, and the foldable form factor, with its bigger screens and split-view multitasking, is where that kind of AI is most visible. The lineup ships with Gemini Intelligence integration and free trials of Google's premium AI tier, and the company frames the foldable's large screen as the natural home for on-device assistants that need room to show their work. Whether that is a real advantage or marketing, it is the argument every phone maker is now making, and Samsung is making it on the form factor with the most screen.

The context for the launch is a market that has consolidated around foldables as the premium differentiator. Apple's entry into the category is expected to land this year, which every foldable maker has been anticipating, and Samsung's decision to stack its lineup so high now, with an Ultra tier, a camera that matches its flagship flat phones and a thinner chassis, reads as a defence of the high ground before the newcomer arrives. The timing, a full two months before the expected Apple event, is not accidental.

The honest assessment is that the Ultra is a landmark device in a category that has finally found its footing, and that the foldable market's real test is still ahead. Samsung has sold foldables in volume for years, but the premium tier has been a two-horse race, and the arrival of Apple will change the economics of the whole category. For now, though, the story is simple: the most capable folding phone ever built is shipping, the reviews are strong, and the question that mattered for seven years, whether anyone would actually buy these things, has been answered. The new question, whether foldables become the default flagship form factor, is being posed by this lineup, and the answer will come from the sales charts.
