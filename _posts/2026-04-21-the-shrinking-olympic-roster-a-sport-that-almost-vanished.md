The IOC published its final decision on the 2032 summer programme this week, and the deletions were the news: four sports cut outright, several more reduced to skeleton programmes, and one of the movement's oldest disciplines, modern pentathlon, left with a single event and a reprieve that its federations describe as a warning. The cuts were framed in the familiar language of modernisation, of youth appeal, of television ratings and city costs, and the framing was accurate as far as it went. The details suggest a more careful story about which sports the Olympics can still afford, and about what the cost of the programme is actually protecting.

The arithmetic of the decision is the part that the press releases leave out. The Olympics are no longer funded by the broadcast deals of the last century; the modern Games run on sponsorship, on host-city contributions and on a programme that the IOC controls with an eye to the margins. Each sport on the roster carries costs, venues, officials, housing, insurance, and each carries a revenue contribution that is measured in minutes of primetime. The sports cut this week, the shooting-heavy disciplines, the low-viewership team events, the combined competitions whose formats the casual viewer found confusing, were the ones whose cost per broadcast minute had drifted furthest from the sports that fill the stadiums. The IOC is not deleting history; it is rebalancing a portfolio.

The human cost of the decision is real, and it deserves to be named without sentiment. Every cut ends careers that were built to an Olympic timetable, closes the pathway for the athletes who trained in the deleted disciplines, and empties the federations whose entire existence was organized around a four-year cycle. The athletes' statements this week have been dignified and specific, and the specificity, the training hours, the funding streams, the national programmes that will close, is the part that the modernisation language cannot absorb. The Games will survive the cuts; the communities that built the deleted sports will not, and the losses will be carried quietly by the people who made the Olympic dream their profession.

I have two competing reactions to the decision, and I think both are legitimate. The first is that the Games are a public institution and the roster is a public question, and that an unelected committee in Lausanne deciding which sports deserve the world's attention is exactly the kind of authority that should be exercised carefully, with the interests of the athletes at the centre. The second is that the Olympic programme has always been a negotiated compromise between tradition and the brutal economics of a quadrennial spectacle, and that a movement which refuses to make the cuts will eventually lose the cities, the sponsors and the broadcasters that fund everything else. Both reactions can be true, and the decision is the point where they meet.

The middle ground, which I suspect the next decade will be forced to explore, is a programme that changes shape gradually, with transition funds for the affected federations and a review cycle that treats every sport's place as temporary. The modern pentathlon's single event is a start in that direction, a holding position rather than a deletion, and its future will be decided by whether its federations can rebuild around the new format. The Olympic movement is doing what every successful institution eventually does, which is to cut what it can no longer carry while claiming to modernise. The claim deserves its scepticism; the cut, on the numbers, was probably necessary.
