The annual meeting of the world's climate diplomacy opened this month in Belém, a port city at the mouth of the Amazon, and the choice of location was never going to be neutral. Every COP carries the symbolism of its host; this one carries more than most. Belém is the gateway to the largest rainforest on Earth, the biome whose fate is bound up with the global carbon cycle and whose protection is simultaneously a national project and a planetary one. Holding the summit here, after years in which the Amazon burned in the world's headlines, was a statement before a single speech had been delivered: the region at the centre of the climate story would also host the meeting about it.

The opening phase of the summit, the leaders' segment held in the first days, set the tone. The speeches were the usual mix of ambition and conditionality, pledges to accelerate, requests for money, reminders of commitments made at previous meetings. Beneath the rhetoric sat the practical agenda that the two weeks of negotiations would have to carry: the gap between what countries have promised and what their actual plans imply, the unfinished business of climate finance, and the machinery of implementation, the carbon markets, the transparency rules, the adaptation programmes, that translate pledges into measurable change. The summit's own branding emphasised delivery, and delivery is precisely the part that has historically been hardest.

The finance question dominated the corridors. The previous year's meeting had agreed a new collective goal for climate finance, and the question now was whether the money would actually move, and at what speed, and with what conditions. For the host region the question is not abstract: the Amazonian states and the countries around them sit at the intersection of the largest carbon sink and the strongest development pressures, and the sum they need to protect the forest while growing their economies is measured in sums that the current flows do not reach. The negotiations over money, always the hardest in any COP, were expected to run late.

There was also the matter of the summit's own contradiction, which the location made hard to ignore. The delegates arrived by air, the city's infrastructure strained under the influx, and the meeting's own footprint was not trivial; the COP that discussed the Amazon was also a generator of emissions on a scale that a small country might envy. The organisers had made gestures toward offsetting and local procurement, but the fundamental awkwardness of a global aviation-fuelled conference on a rainforest riverbank was not something any agenda could dissolve.

What the summit could do, and what the opening week did do, was restate the terms. The science has not changed, the temperature record of the past year was another reminder that the window is narrowing, and the negotiations in Belém were, by their own framing, about the machinery of the next decade: how fast the energy transition moves, how the forests are paid for, how the poorest countries are protected. The Amazon hosted the conversation about all of it, and the outcome, whatever the final text says, will be measured less by the words agreed than by what the money actually does afterwards.
