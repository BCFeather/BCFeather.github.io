Somewhere toward the end of the month the light gives up a few more minutes, and the change is too small to point to on any given day. It is only when one compares one evening to the evening a month ago that the difference becomes obvious: the sun sets noticeably earlier, the commute home is already in twilight, and the city has turned its lights on earlier than it remembers doing. No single evening announces this. It is the accumulation of small losses, one or two minutes a day, that finally crosses the threshold where it can be felt.

The first sign is at the hour that used to be the brightest part of the afternoon. In early September the late afternoon was still long and golden, the kind of light that lets a person run an errand outside without thinking. By the end of the month that same hour has softened and shortened, and the shadows have crept east. One walks out of a building expecting daylight and finds a dusk that was not there last week. The body adjusts before the mind does, reaching for a layer, noticing that the evening chill arrives earlier, switching on the lamp at a desk while the afternoon is technically still going.

What is odd is how little else has changed. It is not cold. The trees have not turned. The air, at midday, can still be warm. The seasonal change that is happening is invisible to everything except the clock of the sky. There is no frost to mark it, no first fire, no decisive date. It is purely the withdrawal of light, a quiet contraction that the body registers as a slight shift in mood before the mind names it as autumn.

The city rearranges itself around this. The evening exercise that happened after work in August now has to be done in the dark, or moved earlier. The walk along the river, which at this time of year used to finish in daylight, now finishes under streetlights. Cafés turn their heaters on the first evening that feels like it, and the outdoor tables, which were the whole point of summer, start to empty as the dark comes in. None of this is dramatic. It is the slow, unpanicked adaptation of a city to a shorter afternoon.

There is a particular melancholy to this stretch that is not quite sadness. It is the sense of a chapter closing. The long light of summer, which felt as though it would never end, has been ending for weeks without anyone remarking, and now the ending is undeniable. The days are drawing in, and they will keep drawing in for months, until the solstice. For now, though, the loss is gentle. There is still warmth in the middle of the day, still green on the trees, still enough evening to sit outside for a little while.

The people who live with the seasons learn not to fight this. They put on the layer, they move the walk earlier, they light the lamp while the afternoon is still going. They do not mourn the light that has gone, because they know it will come back. But they notice it, the way one notices the shortening of a holiday that is nearly over: not with alarm, but with a quiet, attentive regret for the hour that is gone, and the hour that is coming, and the particular, unrepeatable quality of this light, which will not return for another year.
