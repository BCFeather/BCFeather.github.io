---

The restructuring memos went out this week across Silicon Valley's software companies, and the language of each one was carefully identical, 'realignment', 'focus', 'efficiency', with the numbers attached in the small print. The combined cuts are in the thousands, concentrated in the functions that software companies used to hire without thinking: the customer support tiers, the project managers, the internal tooling teams, the layers of middle management that grew during the boom years. The same companies are posting record revenues and record profit margins, which is exactly the point, and the layoffs are not a sign of decline. They are the first visible cost of a decade of automation arriving all at once.

The structural explanation is uncomfortable because it is simple. The software industry has spent ten years building tools that automate its own operations, the support chatbots that answer the routine tickets, the agents that triage the internal requests, the code assistants that let a smaller team ship what a larger one used to ship. The tools were adopted first in other industries, and then, as the marginal cost of using them fell below the marginal cost of a salary, they were adopted at home. The current wave of cuts is the industry eating its own product, and the executives writing the memos are, with a straight face, citing the productivity gains that the memos themselves made possible.

What makes this cycle different from the layoffs of the dot-com bust is that the revenue is real. The companies are not cutting because they are failing; they are cutting because the revenue can now be produced by fewer people, and the difference between the two reasons changes everything about the aftermath. A failing industry lays off and shrinks; a profitable industry lays off and expands its margins, which is why the stock prices rose on the announcements. The market is rewarding the companies for discovering how much of their headcount was, in the new economics, optional.

The human geography of the cuts deserves more attention than the stock reaction. The layoffs are landing on the people who were hired during the boom, the support engineers, the program managers, the specialists whose skills were commoditised by the very tools their employers sell. Their severance is generous by national standards and thin by the cost of living in the bay, and the resumes circulating this month are the first census of a workforce that the industry trained for jobs it has now automated. The retraining programmes announced alongside the cuts, the 'internal mobility' programmes, the 'AI literacy' courses, are real and small, and the gap between their rhetoric and their capacity is the quiet scandal of this cycle.

The valley is doing something historically unprecedented, which is to automate its own workforce while it is still growing, and the experiment will be studied for decades regardless of how it turns out. The optimists read the cuts as the sign of an industry getting leaner and more productive; the pessimists read them as the first instalment of a structural adjustment that has no end point, because the tools keep improving and the headcount targets will keep being revised. Both readings are available this week, and the truth will be visible in the next hiring cycle, when the same companies either start adding people in new roles or quietly discover that they do not need to. The second act of Silicon Valley has begun with a memo, and the memo, for once, is honest about the economics that wrote it.
