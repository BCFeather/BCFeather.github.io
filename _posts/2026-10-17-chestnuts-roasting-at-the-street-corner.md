There is a smell that arrives in the city only once the air has properly turned. It is sweet and smoky and a little burnt, and it comes from the corner where the chestnut seller parks his cart each afternoon. For weeks you walk past without noticing. Then one evening you catch it from half a block away, and you know, without checking a calendar, that autumn has arrived.

The stall is not much to look at. A metal cart, a round iron pan half-full of dark, slit shells rattling over a low heat, a stack of paper bags, a pair of tongs, and a small light that comes on as the dusk thickens. The man who runs it is there from the cold of the afternoon through the early evening, and the business is small and constant. People stop on the way home, hold out a phone or a few coins, take a paper bag, and walk on peeling shells as they go.

The chestnut is the right food for this season in a way that needs no explaining. It is hot, which matters when the evening is drawing cold. It is messy, which makes you slow down. It comes in a paper bag you hold against your palm, so the warmth travels up into your hands even after the nuts are eaten. There is nothing efficient about it. You stand on the corner for a minute, shelling, burning your fingertips a little, and the rush of the day loosens.

What the stall really sells is a small, cheap certainty. The supermarkets in the basement of the new malls sell everything, but they do not sell this. A paper bag of hot chestnuts is the same price it has been for years, the same smell, the same slightly unpredictable roast, and it appears at the same corner in the same week every autumn. In a season when the weather is the only thing reliably changing, that small constancy is its own comfort.

The ritual has its own unspoken rules. You wait your turn. You do not rush the man at the pan. You take the bag while it is still too hot to hold comfortably. You walk home with one hand in the bag, shell to shell, dropping the dark husks into the pocket or the bin, and by the time you reach the door the bag is empty and your fingers smell of chestnut for the rest of the night.

These stalls are older than the street they stand on. The city has rebuilt itself around them, the shop fronts have changed hands, the chain stores have come and gone, and the chestnut cart has kept its corner. It is one of the last street foods that has not been organized into a brand. There is no app for it, no loyalty scheme, no photograph-worthy presentation. There is only the smell, the pan, and the queue of people who have smelled it too.

When the days shorten and the light goes early, the small warm things matter more than they do in summer. A paper bag of roasted chestnuts is one of them. It lasts a few minutes, costs little, and marks the season more reliably than any forecast. The corner smells of smoke and sugar, the pan turns, and autumn, for another year, is officially here.
