On the first of September, a transition that happens every year, everywhere in the country, begins again with undiminished force: the school year opens. The streets that all summer had the easy tempo of holidays regain their early-morning purpose; the crossings acquire their uniformed wardens; the shops that sold nothing but cold drinks begin to sell pencil cases and water bottles. The change is so universal and so regular that it is easy to treat as scenery, but it is one of the great recurring logistics operations of a modern society: tens of millions of children, reinserted into the same building, on the same day, all over again.

The morning itself has a texture worth noticing. The first-day rituals are repeated in every household with variations too numerous to count and a structure too regular to miss: the alarm set earlier than it has been all summer, the uniform checked for growth, the bag packed and repacked, the photo at the gate that some children endure and others enjoy. The families that perform these rituals are doing something older than any institution: handing a child, again, to the care of a school, and trusting that the system on the other side of the gate is ready for them.

The schools are ready, which is itself an achievement. The maintenance crews that repainted the corridors, the teachers who spent the last week in meetings and classroom preparations, the administrators who reconciled class lists and timetables, the cooks and the bus drivers and the crossing guards, all of them were on the move in August so that the first of September could look effortless. The opening of a school year is the moment when a whole chain of invisible summer work becomes visible as a single ordinary morning. The count of buildings, classrooms, desks and books involved is itself national in scale, and the coordination behind a single morning, the bus routes that appear exactly where they did last year, the timetables that fit together like a solved puzzle, is one of the quiet achievements of the administrative year, repeated annually and noticed, if at all, only when it fails.

There is a particular weight in the firsts. For the youngest children, the first of September is the first real separation, the day the world widens beyond the family and the authority of the teacher joins the authority of the parent. For the oldest, it may be the beginning of the final year, the countdown to the examinations that will decide the next stage. The same date carries both: the first day and the last first day, separated by a dozen Septembers, all of them opening with the same bell.

The school year is the calendar that many lives actually run on, more than the civil one. Its turning points, the opening, the holidays, the examinations, the closing, structure childhood, parenthood, and in a smaller way adult life, for years at a time, and the first of September is the hinge on which that calendar swings. The gates open, the mornings change, the year's work begins, and the country, without announcing it, has completed another of its oldest and most reliable transitions. The new school year is not a new beginning, exactly. It is the same beginning, renewed, as it has been every year, and as it will be again.
