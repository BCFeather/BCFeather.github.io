---

There is a sentence that appears, in one form or another, in the mission statements of almost every large technology company: we compete for your attention. It is not a metaphor anymore. Attention has a price, a market and a measurement apparatus. Every scroll, every pause, every half-second of dwell time is logged, weighted, auctioned and fed back into the design of the next screen, and the whole apparatus is so efficient that a person who spends an evening online has effectively performed several hours of unpaid work for the attention economy, with the product being their own state of mind.

It would be easy to write this off as a conspiracy of manipulators, and some of it is. But the honest version of the story is more uncomfortable, because it implicates everyone. The attention economy exists because information outgrew the human capacity to process it, and something had to ration our exposure. Recommendation systems, autoplay, infinite feeds and notification badges are, at bottom, allocation mechanisms, and they allocate the one resource that cannot be manufactured. A search engine that answers a question in seconds, a feed that surfaces exactly the article you would have wanted, a video that starts precisely when the last one ends: these are conveniences that no earlier generation had, and giving them up is harder than the critics admit.

The cost is real, and it shows up in places we can measure. Sustained concentration is becoming a specialist skill. Deep reading, the kind that requires holding an argument in mind for more than a few pages, is increasingly confined to the classroom, while the reading people actually do in their spare time has fragmented into items measured in seconds. The metrics that govern online content reward the immediately graspable, the mildly surprising, the emotionally mobilising, and punish the slow, the complex and the hedged. In one sense this is a market clearing efficiently: the medium has discovered what most people, left to their own devices, choose. In another, it is a market deciding something it was never asked to decide, which is what a society's collective attention should be spent on.

The subtle part is that nobody is forcing anyone. An evening of short videos is chosen, moment by moment, by the same person who complains that they cannot focus on a book. This is not hypocrisy; it is the signature of an environment optimised against a weakness that every human shares. The recommendation engine does not need to be malicious to be effective, it only needs to be better at predicting your next impulse than you are at resisting it. Framing the problem as a battle of willpower against apps is the framing that the apps themselves prefer, because it locates the fault in the user and absolves the architecture.

If there is a way out, it is probably not a ban or a cleanse but an awareness of what the exchange actually is. Every minute given to a feed is a minute spent inside someone else's model of you, and the model is optimised for retention, not for your life. The people who do best against the attention economy tend to be the ones who have simply noticed this: they read long things on purpose, they let feeds go unopened for days, they treat their own focus as a budget rather than an infinite resource. Attention is a currency, and like any currency it can be hoarded, invested or squandered. The market would prefer that you spend it; nothing stops you from holding it for something that deserves it.
