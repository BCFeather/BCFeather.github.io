The negotiations over the tariff programme moved into the diplomatic channels in mid-April, and the talks acquired the rhythm that such negotiations always develop: the officials meeting in the cities that the trade diplomacy has used for generations, the sessions conducted behind the closed doors, the statements issued through the spokespeople, and the quiet progress that the headlines rarely capture. The trade conflict that the year opened has reached the stage where the instruments, the tariffs and the countermeasures, have been deployed, and the deployment's purpose, the negotiation, has begun in earnest; the room where the talks are conducted never empties, because the talks are the place where the conflict will actually be settled.

The structure of the negotiations is the part that the participants have learned to manage with the care that the stakes require. The talks are conducted in the layers that the trade diplomacy has built: the technical sessions where the officials work through the products and the rates, the ministerial meetings where the principles are settled, and the leaders' calls where the final terms are exchanged. The layers allow the progress to be made without the commitments being locked too early, and the sequencing is the trade negotiation's oldest trick; the trick is the reason the talks can continue even when the public statements are at their most confrontational.

The participants' positions, as the talks began, were the positions that the year's conflict had established. The American negotiators carried the administration's doctrine, the renegotiation of the terms on the American side of the ledger, with the tariffs as the instrument and the pauses as the incentive; the partners carried the defence of their industries and their sovereignty, with the countermeasures as the leverage and the exemptions as the goal. The two positions were far apart at the talks' opening, and the distance was the negotiation's starting point; the work of the sessions is the narrowing of the distance, product by product, sector by sector, and the narrowing is the slow work that the trade diplomacy has perfected over the decades.

The economic stakes of the talks are the part that the negotiators carry into the room. The tariffs that the pause has suspended would, if applied in full, raise the costs across the global supply chains, redirect the trade flows, and test the inflation targets that the central banks have been defending; the negotiators on both sides know the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the pressure that the talks operate under. The sectors that the tariffs would hit have their own representatives in the capitals, and the representatives' voices are the domestic dimension that the negotiators must balance against the international one; the balance is the trade negotiation's permanent difficulty, and the difficulty is the reason the room never empties.

The outlook for the talks is the part that the analysts have refused to predict, and the refusal is the honest position. The pauses provide the calendar, the ninety days that end in the summer; the incentives provide the structure, the reduced rates for the partners that accept the terms; and the politics provide the uncertainty, the domestic pressures, the electoral calendars, the leaders' prerogatives that the negotiations cannot fully control. The talks in Geneva and the other cities will run through the spring, with the sessions, the statements and the deadlines accumulating, and the outcome, when it comes, will be the settlement that the year's conflict has been working toward, or the escalation that the settlement's failure would open. The room never empties, and the work of the room is the work that will decide the year's trade politics.
