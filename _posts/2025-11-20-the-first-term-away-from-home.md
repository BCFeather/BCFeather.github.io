Three months into my first term at university, the city has stopped being an event and become a place where I live. The famous buildings no longer make me look up every time I pass. The metro map is no longer a maze, just a set of lines I have memorised by need. I know which cafeteria window serves the food I can afford and which corner of the library stays quiet in the afternoon. The novelty has worn off, and in its place something more honest has arrived: routine, homesickness, and the first evidence of the person I am going to be here.

The dormitory is where the education actually happens, and it is not on the syllabus. Four people from four provinces in one room, with different accents, different bedtimes and different ideas about what 'clean enough' means. The first month was a careful diplomacy of boundaries, who uses the desk, who keeps the light on, who talks to their family at night. By now we have settled into the kind of peace that comes from knowing each other's habits well enough to stop noticing them. I have learned more about the geography of China from my roommates than from any atlas, and most of it is about food.

The dialect question is the one I keep coming back to. My roommates and I all speak Mandarin to each other, and we all switch languages the moment a phone call home comes in. The girl from Sichuan goes soft and quick. The boy from Shandong slows down into something rounder. And I, from Anhui, feel my voice change without deciding to, slipping back into the tones of home. We joke about it, calling each other's dialects secret codes. Under the joke is a real discovery: the accent we smoothed over for the exam is still the language we think in, and no classroom ever told us that.

The studying is harder than the brochures suggested, in the way that everything real is harder than its brochure. The lectures are crowded, the reading lists are long, and the professors do not slow down for anyone. I have had weeks where I understood maybe two thirds of what was being said, and weeks where the two thirds was on a good day. The temptation is to blame the course, the teacher, the system. The more honest explanation is that I am learning how to learn in a place with no one watching over my shoulder, and that skill turns out to be the actual subject of the first year.

I have also started to understand something about home that I could not see from inside it. The town I left was not a backdrop but an argument, made of particular people and particular expectations, and I carried the argument with me. When I decided things here, I could hear it. Which subject to pursue, how late to stay out, whether to spend the money or save it. Some of the argument I have accepted and made my own. Some of it I have started to argue with. That process, slow and mostly internal, is the part of university no one photographs.

The homesickness arrives on schedules I cannot predict, usually in the evenings, usually with a smell or a sound as the trigger, a certain way the canteen stir-fries the greens, a phrase in a stranger's accent. I do not fight it. Missing home is the tuition I pay for having one, and the payment is reasonable. I call my grandmother and she asks if I am eating, and I tell her yes, and it is true enough.

The term is more than half over. The city is ordinary now, the roommates are family-adjacent, and the work is simply the work. I came here expecting to be transformed by knowledge, and instead I have been transformed by the ordinary: by four people in one room, by a dialect I keep, by the daily management of my own time. The transformation is quieter than advertised, and I am starting to think it is the real one.
