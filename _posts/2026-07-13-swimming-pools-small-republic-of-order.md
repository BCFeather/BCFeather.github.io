---

The public swimming pool is one of the few places in a July city where strangers agree on the rules without being asked. You arrive hot and bothered, pay, turn the corner, and the sound changes: the street's traffic drops to a hum behind glass, the air becomes thick and chlorinated, and a different set of conventions takes over. Nobody explains them. You simply fall in with them, the way you fall in with the traffic on the other side of the road.

The rules are small. There is a clock above the far end of the pool, and it is always on. There are lanes, and each lane has a speed, and you are expected to choose the right one. If you are overtaken from behind, you move to the slower lane. If you are faster, you wait at the wall rather than crab past. A black line runs along the bottom of each lane so you can look down and swim straight. A rope separates the fast swimmers from the family splashers, and nobody crosses it. A lifeguard sits on a high chair, and the lifeguard's whistle is, without argument, law.

What is striking is how completely this small republic works. People who on the street jostle and honk and ignore each other become, at the water's edge, considerate and methodical. They queue for the shower. They dry their feet before walking across the tiles. They pull goggles down without being told. The water does this, apparently: it strips away the ordinary city hurry and replaces it with a slower, more deliberate kind of attention. You cannot rush a length. You cannot argue with the clock.

The order is also democratic in a particular way. At the deep end, under the water, nobody can hear your job title or your accent. You are a body moving through water, and the only thing that matters is whether you can hold a pace. A retired engineer and a teenager on holiday share a lane and take it in turn, and there is a small, wordless courtesy between them that no office will ever produce. The pool is one of the last remaining places in the city where equality is not a slogan but a practical arrangement: the water does not care who you are.

There is a quieter side to it, too. The regular swimmers, the ones who come at the same hour every morning, have their own geography of the pool. They know which lane is fastest at seven, which corner is shaded, which locker always sticks. They do not talk much, but they nod. They share a small ritual that the occasional visitor only half sees, the way you half see the regulars in a late-night convenience store. The pool is, for them, an appointment with themselves, and the order of the place is what makes the appointment possible.

By late afternoon the pool fills with children, and the careful lane discipline loosens into splashing and shouting. The shallow end becomes a small, loud republic of its own, with its own arguments about who jumped in first. The lifeguard's whistle is heard more often. Nobody minds. The pool has given up being a serious place for a few hours and become a summer one, which is also a kind of order, just a louder one.

When you finally climb out, the city outside is still hot. But you carry something of the pool with you for a while: a steadier breathing, a slower step, the memory of the clock at the end of the lane. The small republic closes at nine, and the lanes drain, and the water goes still. In the morning it will refill, and the regulars will return, and the order will assemble itself again, without anyone having to explain.
