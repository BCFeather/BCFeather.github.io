The announcement that new tariffs on the country's two land neighbours would take effect on 1 February landed on the final weekend of January, and the date, chosen and announced without consultation, had the force of a deadline imposed from nowhere. The two capitals that had spent the previous weeks negotiating with the new administration responded with the mixture of defiance and calculation that the moment required: the public statements promised retaliation, and the private channels, the trade officials reported, reopened within hours to talk about what could be offered in exchange for a delay. The tariff, if it takes effect, will be the first test of the new trade doctrine, and the test is being conducted in the full glare of the markets.

The economics of the proposed levies are the part that the coverage keeps circling, and the economics are genuinely unusual. The neighbours are the largest trading partners of the country that threatened them, their economies are deeply integrated with its supply chains, and the tariffs, at the rates announced, would raise the prices of the goods that cross the borders in both directions. The standard argument for such tariffs, that they protect domestic industry, applies weakly to the goods actually traded; the argument that the tariffs are a bargaining instrument, applied to obtain concessions on migration and the border, applies more strongly, and the choice between the two readings is the choice that the markets have been making all week, with the currency and the bond prices moving on every statement.

The politics of the deadline are as significant as the economics. The announcement was made in the language of the campaign trail, and the language carried into the government with the transition: the border as the first priority, the neighbours as the source of the problems, and the tariffs as the instrument that would force the solutions. The neighbours' responses were carefully calibrated to the politics: the statements emphasised the shared interests and the shared economies, the willingness to work with the new administration, and the quiet insistence that the tariffs would hurt the American consumers as much as the foreign producers. The calibration reflects the calculation that the negotiations will succeed, and the calculation will be tested on 1 February.

The markets' reaction to the deadline has been the honest measure of how seriously the threat is taken. The currencies of the two neighbours fell against the dollar in the week of the announcement, the equity markets priced in the sectors that would be most affected, and the bond markets began to price the inflation that the tariffs would bring if they take effect. The trading desks have learned, in the years of trade conflict, to distinguish the threats that are real from the threats that are theatre, and the 1 February deadline, with its specific date and its specific rates, sits on the real side of the line. The distinction, the analysts note, is itself part of the strategy: the threats that the market takes seriously are the threats that extract concessions.

The deadline arrived early in the sense that the negotiation was still in its first weeks, and the early arrival is the point of the exercise. The new administration has chosen to establish the terms of the trade relationship with its neighbours in the first month of its term, to test the instrument of the tariff before the year's other battles, and to make the neighbours' concessions, if they come, in the context of the first hundred days. The negotiations will continue through the weekend; the deadline will arrive on 1 February with the certainty of a date on a calendar and the uncertainty of everything else; and the outcome, whichever way it goes, will set the template for the trade policy of the year.
