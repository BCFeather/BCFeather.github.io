A Chinese-led team has unveiled a science programme that proposes to fill the Earth-Moon region with a constellation of small satellites. Announced at the Tiandu International Conference in early September, the plan would deploy thirty 12U cubesats, satellites roughly the size of a microwave oven, into the region between Earth and the Moon by 2030, with most of the fleet riding a lunar transfer orbit that the designers call an 'ice-cream cone' orbit. The ambition is to use cheap, standardized hardware to do science that has so far required flagship observatories.

The science case is specific and demanding. Two of the headline payloads are a gamma-ray burst locator, designed to find short gamma-ray bursts quickly and localize them to sub-arcsecond accuracy, and an all-sky survey instrument that would map the sky in a band where the constellation's geometry gives it an advantage. The argument is that a widely distributed set of small satellites can monitor transient events across the whole sky in ways that a single large telescope, pointed in one direction at a time, cannot. It is the same logic that moved astronomy toward constellations of small spacecraft, and the Earth-Moon region is a place where that logic has not yet been applied.

The engineering details are where the programme shows its character. The satellites will be deployed in six batches, in the 1:3 resonance orbit, a path designed to spread the fleet evenly around the region while keeping each satellite cheap to build and launch. The programme's organisers describe the terms as 'free to fly, self-funded': partners can join with their own payloads without paying for rideshare, as long as they cover their own costs and agree to share data openly. The operational and communications systems will be provided by China's lunar exploration and deep-space network, which is what makes the open-join model practical, since new partners do not need to build ground infrastructure.

The international angle is the programme's most notable feature. The announcement came at a conference that drew the International Astronomical Union and the UN Office for Outer Space Affairs, and the constellation is explicitly framed as an international science programme with Chinese coordination. The model, share rides and ground support in exchange for open science, is a familiar one in astronomy, but applying it to the Moon region at this scale is new. It also signals where the coming decade's lunar infrastructure is heading: the region will be crowded with activity, and constellations like this one are part of the traffic, watching it and being watched.

The sceptical reading is worth keeping. Thirty cubesats by 2030 is a schedule that depends on launch availability, partner funding and the reliability of cheap satellites in a harsh radiation environment, and science constellations of this ambition have a history of slipping. The gamma-ray burst science also competes with dedicated missions that already exist or are planned. But the point of the programme is not only the science. It is an infrastructure statement: that the Earth-Moon region is becoming a place where nations cooperate in orbit, where standardized small satellites can participate, and where the supply chain for lunar activity, launches, ground networks, rideshares, is being built now. The constellation is the science that rides on that statement, and it is worth watching for both reasons.
