By almost any measure, 2025 was the year SpaceX's Starship, the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown, finally began to behave like a mature programme, and it did so in the least smooth way possible. The year opened with the most dramatic failure in the vehicle's short history, when the ship on the seventh test flight broke apart shortly after launch, raining debris over the Caribbean and triggering a brief grounding. It closed with the booster and ship being caught or recovered with a regularity that would have been unthinkable twelve months earlier.

The January explosion was the kind of event that makes headlines and, for a while, threatens a programme. The ship came apart minutes into flight, littering the region with wreckage and forcing regulators to step in. The response was instructive. Rather than defend the failure, the company treated it as data: the anomaly was traced to a vibration-induced problem in the upper stage, the booster's role in a failed attempt to re-enter the atmosphere was reworked, and the programme resumed test flights within months, each one pushing further than the last.

What followed was a sequence of recoveries that changed the narrative. Booster catches at the launch tower, which had been a single spectacular success in 2024, became the expected outcome. Orbital burns and re-entry attempts progressed, and by the autumn the vehicle was no longer a testbed for its own survival but a platform being prepared for the missions that actually matter: propellant transfer, orbital refuelling, and, eventually, the landings on the Moon that NASA's Artemis programme depends on.

The year is worth reading as a lesson in how hard this particular frontier really is. The explosion was not a malfunction within a mature system but a routine step in a development programme that tests by flying, and every failure narrowed the unknown. There is a temptation to treat each mishap as proof of recklessness and each success as proof of inevitability; both readings miss the point. The method, for better and worse, is to break things in controlled conditions until the design holds, and the method is working, in the sense that the vehicle is measurably closer to operational use.

The stakes are larger than the company. Starship is the only vehicle in development that can carry the tonnage required for crewed lunar missions in the current architecture, and the programme's health is bound up with the health of the Artemis timetable. That coupling is a risk as much as a capability. One rocket, however powerful, is a single point of failure for an entire national space ambition, and the industry has tied an enormous amount to a vehicle that spent a year demonstrating, in public, how hard it is to leave the ground. It is succeeding; it is also a reminder that there are no shortcuts to orbit.
