Most exoplanets are found indirectly, by the wobble they tug on their star or the faint dip of light as they pass in front of it. Direct imaging, actually taking a picture of the planet's own glow, is far harder, because the star is millions of times brighter and drowns its companion out. On 15 July the European Southern Observatory announced a new direct image that pushes the technique to its faintest limit: a third giant planet, orbiting the young star Beta Pictoris, has been photographed after more than a decade of hiding in the data.

The star is a favourite for this kind of work. Beta Pictoris is only about twenty million years old and lies just sixty-three light-years away, close enough and young enough that its planets are still warm and glowing. Two gas giants, Beta Pictoris b and c, were already known, together with a dust disc and exocomets. The new world, Beta Pictoris d, is the inner and faintest member of the family. Estimated at about 2.4 times the mass of Jupiter, it is roughly a hundred times fainter than its larger sibling b and ranks among the least massive worlds ever imaged from the ground. Its light shows an effective temperature around 600 kelvin and signs of methane in its atmosphere.

The detective story is as good as the astronomy. Once the team spotted the new object with the ERIS infrared camera on the Very Large Telescope, they dug back through archives stretching eleven years, using the older SPHERE instrument on the same telescope and NIRCam on the James Webb Space Telescope. The planet was there all along, in images from 2014 onwards, but had been too faint to recognize until ERIS's greater sensitivity made it obvious. Mapping its orbit from that long baseline placed it about twenty-six astronomical units from its star, close to the orbit of Neptune and near the inner edge of the system's dust disc. The paper, by Ben Sutlieff and colleagues, appears in the *Astrophysical Journal Letters*.

The significance is partly about the instrument. Direct imaging has been limited to the very brightest, most massive young planets, the low-hanging fruit of the neighbourhood. Pushing down to a 2.4-Jupiter world means the technique is reaching planets that look more like the real population. Beta Pictoris is now only the second system, after HR 8799, where more than two planets have been directly observed, which lets astronomers watch gravity at work in a real multi-planet architecture.

The larger meaning is about how discovery happens. This world was not found by a new survey; it was hiding in ten years of existing observations, waiting for a better camera. It is a reminder that archives hold a great deal that has not yet been looked at properly, and that the next generation of instruments, above all the Extremely Large Telescope under construction in Chile, will be re-reading data sets that already exist and pulling out objects no one flagged.

The limits are in the method. A directly imaged planet is seen in infrared glow, so mass and temperature are models rather than measurements, and only young, hot worlds show up this way. Cold, rocky, Earth-sized planets around quiet stars remain beyond reach. But as a demonstration of what the cameras can now see, the faint third world around Beta Pictoris is a sign that the picture of other solar systems is about to get a good deal more crowded.
