The dry spell had lasted long enough that the city had started to look held together by dust. The plane trees along the main road had gone brown at the edges, the pavements shed a fine pale grit with every footstep, and the air at midday still carried the last temper of summer. Then one morning, without much warning, it began to rain, and the city changed its mind.

Autumn rain is not like the rain of any other season. Summer rain arrives in a rush, a downpour that clears the air in half an hour and is gone, leaving the steam off the hot asphalt and the smell of wet concrete. Autumn rain arrives slowly, as if it has all the time in the world. It does not fall so much as settle, a fine, persistent curtain that comes down for hours and then for a day, and never quite decides to stop. There is no thunder, no drama. There is only the steady grey and the sound of water on the canopy.

The city answers it quietly. On the first wet morning the usual rush hour thins. People who could have walked take the bus instead, or stay home, and the streets are left to those with no choice. The traffic moves slower, and that slowness spreads: the honking eases, the argument at the crossing dies down, the whole machine seems to run at a lower gear. A city in the rain is a city that has remembered it is made of people, not of throughput.

The details are what stay with you. The shop awnings flipped down over the doorways, each one a small dry island. The umbrella that goes inside out at the corner, and the stranger who helps wrestle it back. The way the wet road turns into a mirror for the lights, so that the evening seems to have twice as many of them. The chestnut seller who has rigged a tarpaulin over his pan and stays in business regardless, the nuts roasting in the damp.

There is a particular light on these days, a low silver that flattens the tall buildings and makes the distant hills, normally invisible behind the haze, come clear for the first time in months. The air that was dusty and still becomes clean and moving, and walking through it is a small physical pleasure that no one mentions. By the second day the gutters are running, the leaves that were hanging on for dear life have let go in handfuls, and the pavement is a soft brown scatter that the rain keeps pressing flat.

People have complicated feelings about this weather. On a dry, warm October weekend, the city is out in force, the parks full, the riverside busy. A wet weekday cuts through all of that and leaves the indoors. Yet there is a comfort in being inside while it rains outside, the cup of tea, the book, the low grey at the window that asks nothing of you. The autumn rain licenses doing nothing, and after a long term that is not nothing at all.

When it finally clears, the world is washed and the light is sharper, and you notice that the trees have turned in a night. The rain was the hinge. It took the city out of the last of summer and set it down, gently, into the autumn.
