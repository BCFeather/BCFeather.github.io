There is a week in early July when the city simply gives in. The mornings start warm instead of cool, the kind of warm that has been building since the night before and will not break until after midnight. People stop arguing about whether it is hot. They arrange themselves around the heat instead, and the city that emerges for the next six weeks is a different place from the one of other months.

The first thing heat does is change the hours. Mornings become the useful part of the day, the only hours when walking outside is not a decision. Shopkeepers sweep their pavements early, before the sun climbs, and commuters move with a quiet efficiency that disappears by noon. Lunchtime becomes a long, low affair, eaten indoors near a fan, nobody in a hurry. The evening is when the city wakes again: streets fill as the light goes gold, dinner is eaten later, children stay out past the hour they are usually called home. The normal clock is overturned by a temperature, and everyone adjusts without being told.

The second thing heat does is reveal what the city is made of. Concrete and asphalt store the day and give it back at night, so the walk home at ten o'clock is still warm from the pavement. Trees, where they are planted in any density, mark the difference between a shaded road and an exposed one like the difference between rooms. Air conditioning changes how buildings meet the street: glass doors that hiss cold air open and shut, and the threshold between outside and inside becomes a small, conditioned event. People learn which sides of which streets to walk on, and which bus stops have any canopy at all.

What strikes you most in a hot July is how much of ordinary life is organized around small, repeated tolerances. The cup of tea that should be cooling by the station. The wait at a crossing that, in winter, would be a few unremarkable seconds, but in heat becomes a decision about whether to cross now or wait for the next green. The queue at a shop that sells cold drinks. Each is trivial on its own, and taken together they add up to a day that has been lengthened and bent by weather. Nobody complains loudly, because complaining would only make the air feel heavier.

There is also a particular social softening. People are slower to argue. Strangers on a bus exchange the kind of small remark about the heat that, in colder months, would be reserved for acquaintances. Neighbours who never speak to each other will comment on the evening's air, on whether it is going to break, on the rain that has not come. The city, which in winter tends to keep its distance, loosens in heat, the way a hand loosens its grip when the room warms up.

None of this is a romance of summer. Heat is also days that do not end, sleep that comes hard, a constant low hum of tiredness that sits under everything. It asks for patience the way a long journey asks for patience: not the heroic kind, but the ordinary kind that tolerates small delays and does not pretend they are not happening. The city, for its part, is not built for this. It is built for a cooler, more efficient version of itself, and July is when that gap shows.

By the end of the month the heat will have settled into something habitual. People will stop noticing the mornings are warm. The long evenings will simply be how life is arranged. Then, slowly, the light will begin to change, and the city will fold itself back into the year. For now, though, there is only the negotiation, repeated each day, with the temperature.
