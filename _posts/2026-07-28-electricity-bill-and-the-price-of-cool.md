The electricity bill always arrives a month late. The heat of July is paid for in August, when the envelope lands on the mat and the household opens it with a low, familiar sense of resignation. The number inside is not really about the summer that just ended. It is about how the household, over six weeks of heat, negotiated with comfort, and what that negotiation cost.

The negotiation is a quiet one. Nobody sits down and decides to run the air conditioning less. It happens in small, repeated adjustments: the thermostat turned up a degree in the afternoon when the room is empty, the unit turned off an hour before bed, the fan kept on through the evening instead of the compressor. The curtains are drawn in the morning against the sun. The freezer door is closed a little faster. The household, without quite deciding, slides into the habits that cooler-climate households learn over a lifetime, and the bill is the ledger of those small compromises.

What the bill makes visible is how recent all of this is. A generation ago, a July city was tolerated rather than overcome. People slept on balconies, fanned themselves, waited for the evening. Air conditioning was the exception, used in cinemas and shops rather than in homes. Now the compressor outside the window is one of the defining sounds of the season, and the question is no longer whether to cool the room but how much, and at what price. The bill is the moment that question is answered.

The price itself has two layers. There is the obvious layer: the kilowatt-hours, the tariff, the rate that goes up in the hottest part of the day when everyone in the district is running their machine. There is a less obvious layer, which is what the cooling is doing to the grid. Every compressor that starts at six in the evening, when people get home and turn the room down, adds a small spike to the demand curve. The grid, and the power stations that must meet that peak, are sized partly for these summer evenings. The bill that arrives in August is the household's small share of that infrastructure.

What strikes you, looking at the number, is how much of modern comfort is electrified. The food that stays cold, the air that stays cool, the water that is pumped and heated, the light that goes on late when the heat keeps everyone awake: all flows through the same meter. In a cooler month the meter hums along in the background and the bill is a formality. In July, it becomes a conversation. People compare notes on whose unit uses how much, on whether the old machine is worth repairing, on whether a fan really is enough.

There is also a quiet fairness argument underneath. The households that can afford to keep the whole flat cold all day are not the ones that feel the bill the hardest. The ones that feel it are the ones that were already running the compressor sparingly, and the bill arrives to tell them that even sparing cooling costs something. Comfort, in a hot city, is not distributed evenly, and the electricity bill is one of the places that uneven distribution shows up, in black and on paper.

By the time the envelope is opened, the worst heat is usually past. The evenings are drawing out again, and the household has, almost without noticing, started leaving the window open at night. The bill is paid, the lessons filed away, and the conversation about the air conditioner moves to next year: whether to upgrade, whether to insulate, whether to finally fix the gap under the door. The heat will come back, and the bill with it. For now the room is cool, the meter is slowing, and the price of a comfortable July is, for another year, settled.
