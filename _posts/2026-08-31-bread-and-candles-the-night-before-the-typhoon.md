By late afternoon the forecasts have stopped hedging. The storm is coming, it is large, and it will arrive overnight. The city absorbs this in the way it absorbs every such August news: not with panic but with a certain practical bustle that begins at the supermarket. The queues form at the checkout, the bread shelves thin out, the bottled water disappears from its stack, and the candles and batteries are lifted off the lower shelves. Nobody is stocking up for an apocalypse. People are buying, methodically, for twenty-four hours without power.

The shopping list is the same as it has been for generations. Bottled water, because the taps may not run. Bread, because the stove may not light. Something tinned that does not need cooking. A few candles, because the streetlights and the phone chargers will fail together. A battery radio, for the households that still own one, and extra batteries for the ones that do not. The items are humble, and the point of buying them is not survival but comfort, the quiet assurance that one will eat and drink and see while the wind does its work outside.

What is noticeable is the mood. There is no arguing at the tills, no scrambling over the last loaf. People wait their turn and remark, to whoever is next, that this one looks like a big one. The cashier, who has done this many Augusts, bags the water with the calm of long practice. A storm is a shared inconvenience, and a shared inconvenience of a certain size loosens the city in a way a shared prosperity never quite manages. Neighbours who have never spoken discuss the shutters. Strangers compare forecasts.

There is an old contract underneath the ritual. The city knows that for all its engineering, its sea walls and its drainage, it is still at the mercy of the weather for a night. The shopping run is the small acknowledgement of that fact, the equivalent of boarding a window. Nobody pretends that bottled water will stop the storm. It simply accepts that the storm will happen, and that one might as well be dry, fed and lit while it does.

Then comes the waiting evening. The wind rises early, rattling the shutters before it is really blowing, and the last errand-runners hurry home. The shops close an hour early, the streets empty, and the traffic lights go out on schedule, as though the city had decided to rest before the storm arrived. Inside, people light a candle for the habit of it even though the power is still on, and listen to the wind test the building the way a hand tests a door.

Morning, when it comes, is a different city. Branches lie across the pavement, awnings have come down, and the streets are quieter than they should be. But the lights are on again by midday, the shops reopen, and the bread restocks. The storm that seemed overnight like the end of the world turns out to have been, once again, a long night.

That is the secret of the whole ritual. The stocking up is not really about surviving. It is about being a city that expects its storms and braces for them together, one basket of water and candles at a time. The typhoon will come and go. The evening before it, every August, belongs to the bread shelves.
