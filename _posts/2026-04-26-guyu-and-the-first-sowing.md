Around 20 April the solar term called Guyu, the rain of grains, arrives, and with it the quiet understanding that spring is nearly over. The last of the spring water falls on a green, warming land, and the farmers say that by now the rains are feeding the seed, that the time for sowing has come. It is the last of the spring seasons, and it carries a different feeling from the ones before it: not the fresh beginning of the new year but the first serious work of it.

Out in the countryside, these are the days when the fields turn over. The soil, softened by weeks of rain, is turned and raked. The first seeds go in, dropped by hand or by drill into rows that run straight up and down the slope. The work is patient in a way that city work no longer is. A seed is buried, and then the farmer waits. There is no dashboard, no feedback loop, no instant confirmation that anything is happening. One walks the field, and the field gives back nothing but mud and green.

What is striking, watching from the edge of the city, is how little this rhythm has changed. The timetable of the seasons was written long before the apartment blocks, and it still runs underneath them. By late April the rivers are high, the markets are selling the first early vegetables, and the older people, who keep the old calendar in their heads, talk about sowing the way other people talk about meetings. The new term has come, and with it a set of expectations that predate electricity.

There is a kind of modesty in the act of sowing that the rest of the year has forgotten. It is a bet placed almost blind. One prepares the ground carefully, chooses the seed, waters it, and then surrenders control. The weather will decide. There is nothing to optimize, no dashboard to watch, only the quiet confidence that, if the soil is ready and the season is right, the seed will do what seeds do. The whole relationship is an act of patience, and the city, which has forgotten how to wait, watches it with something like envy.

The city itself, of course, does not sow. Its inhabitants buy their food in bags, and the farms that grow it are out of sight, on the edges of the maps they never open. Yet the season still touches them: the air smells faintly of turned earth on the mornings when the wind comes from the country, and the fruit stalls fill with the first produce of the new planting year, small and imperfect and genuinely fresh. The rhythm, though hidden, is still underneath everything.

By the end of April, Guyu gives way to the first days of summer, and the sowing season's first rush is over. The seed is in the ground now, and the waiting has begun, the long waiting that a farmer does and a city cannot quite imagine. For one short fortnight, though, the whole countryside is bent over the earth, starting the year again, one small buried hope at a time.
