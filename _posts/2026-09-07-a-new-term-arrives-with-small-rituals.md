The first week of a new term is strange in the way all beginnings are strange. Nothing has actually changed yet. The buildings are the same, the timetable is printed on the same kind of paper, the people in the corridors are the same people. And yet the air in the city has shifted by a few degrees, and everyone can feel it.

The rituals do most of the work. There is the buying of the notebook, an act that is out of all proportion to what it will later contain. For a day or two the book is blank and therefore perfect, and a surprising amount of hope gets invested in it. There is the choosing of the seat, which in the first week is a genuine decision and in the third week has hardened into property. There is the walking of the route from the gate to the classroom, walked a little faster than usual at first, as if speed could shorten the distance to being settled again.

Nobody talks about how artificial all of this is. That is part of the point. The rituals are a small, agreed performance of seriousness, and the performance is what makes the seriousness feel real. A term cannot simply begin. It has to be inaugurated, by sharpened pencils and clean folders and the particular attention people pay to a schedule that has not yet been lived in. By the end of the month the notebook is half full, the seat is unarguable, and the route is walked on autopilot. The inauguration does its job and disappears, which is exactly what rituals are for.

There is a peculiar mood to these days, neither the relief of the holiday nor the grind of the term. It is a held breath. One arrives early on the first morning and is surprised to be early, because one has not yet calibrated how long the journey actually takes. One listens to the introductions and the expectations and the lists, and nods in the right places, and privately keeps a little back, reserving judgement until the real pace of the term declares itself. It always declares itself, usually about ten days in, when the assignments pile up and the held breath lets go.

The city around the campus adjusts too. The cafés refill with people reading schedules on their phones. The stationer's shop, quiet through August, suddenly has a queue. The buses that were nearly empty in August carry standing passengers again, and the standing passengers are mostly the same age, mostly carrying bags that are slightly too new. These are not important observations in themselves. They are the texture of a season, and the season this texture belongs to is the turning of the year from summer to the long, disciplined stretch that runs until winter.

What the rituals are quietly managing is not logistics but uncertainty. A new term is, by definition, unknown. Nobody yet knows which subject will turn out to be the hard one, which classmate will become a friend, which ambition will survive contact with the actual work. The small ceremonies do not remove that uncertainty. They simply give people something orderly to do while it settles. By the time the notebooks are marked and the seats are claimed and the routes are known, the term has begun, and the beginning, which felt so fragile on the first morning, has already become the ordinary ground underfoot.
