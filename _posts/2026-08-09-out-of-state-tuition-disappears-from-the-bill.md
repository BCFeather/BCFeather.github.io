---

For decades, public universities in the United States have priced themselves in two tiers. Residents pay one rate, everyone else pays a marked-up surcharge, sometimes two or three times as much. The surcharge pays the bills, or so the argument goes, and out-of-state students are a revenue stream. On 4 August the University of Alaska's Board of Regents voted to do away with the second tier altogether. Under an expansion of the Alaska Unlocked initiative, non-resident tuition disappears across the system for the academic year beginning in autumn 2027, for undergraduates and graduates alike.

The vote is a gamble on a different theory of the university's finances. The regents' stated hope is that removing the surcharge will lift enrolment, make pricing transparent, and improve retention of the out-of-state students who do come. A flat, simple price is easier to market to a family in the lower forty-eight states than a resident rate with a mysterious multiplier attached. The system has been fighting a quiet decline: small cohorts, a shrinking college-age population in the state, and the slow departure of young people to schools in the Lower 48. Removing the surcharge is an attempt to make Alaska a plausible destination rather than a distant afterthought.

The trade-off is the immediate revenue. Non-resident tuition is real money, and waiving it on paper means giving that up unless enrolment rises enough to compensate. The bet is that volume and retention, not the surcharge per head, are the levers. It is the same logic behind tuition freezes and in-state-tuition policies for national-guard families, taken one step further. Whether it pays off depends on whether enough students actually choose a university in Alaska for the education on offer, not just for the price.

The move also fits a wider pattern in American higher education, where small and regional systems are experimenting wildly to hold enrolment. Flagship universities can raise prices and fill seats on reputation. The smaller, colder, more distant institutions cannot, and so they compete on price and on simplicity. Alaska is an extreme case geographically, which makes the experiment legible: if waiving the surcharge works there, it suggests the surcharge itself was suppressing demand far more than the revenue it bought. If it fails, the system will have spent its cushion to little effect.

There is a social argument underneath the fiscal one. A public university in a small state has always walked a line between serving its residents and running as a business. The surcharge, by pricing outsiders in tiers, quietly treats the university as a revenue unit first. Removing it is a way of saying the institution exists to educate, and the price should reflect that rather than subsidize a gap. Whether the budget allows that generosity is the open question.

The change does not take effect until autumn 2027, which gives the system time to model the enrolment response and to lobby the state legislature for the subsidy that a waiving policy ultimately requires. For now it is a decision of principle more than a proven economics: the surcharge is gone, and the books will have to be balanced another way. Universities across the country will be watching the enrolment numbers closely, because Alaska's experiment is one the rest may soon be forced to run.
