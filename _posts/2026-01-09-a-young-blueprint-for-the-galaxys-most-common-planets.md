Most of the planets in the Galaxy are not like the ones we grew up with. The census of exoplanets, built over three decades of transit and velocity surveys, has been unambiguous: the typical world is smaller than Neptune and larger than Earth, the class astronomers call sub-Neptunes, orbiting close to stars much like the Sun. They are everywhere, and they are almost completely opaque to us. Their interiors are hidden, their compositions debated, and the process that made them so common is still a matter of theory. A paper in the 8 January issue of *Nature* reports a system that may finally show that process in action: a young star with a compact disk that looks like the early stage of exactly this kind of planet system.

The team, including astronomers at Caltech, analysed a disk around a young star using a full nine-year baseline of observations, an unusually long span for a single disk study. The length matters because disks evolve on human timescales as well as astronomical ones: gaps open, material migrates, and a disk photographed today is not the disk it was when first observed. Over nine years, the compact, tightly structured disk showed the signatures of rapid evolution, with material being gathered and sculpted in a way consistent with the formation of a close-in planetary system. The authors read it as a young progenitor, a snapshot of the stage just before the Galaxy's most common planets take their final form.

The significance is that the system sits at the intersection of two knowns and one unknown. It is known that sub-Neptunes are abundant, and it is known that many of them arrived at their present positions through migration, moving inward from where they formed. What has been missing is a clear picture of the disk environment that produces them, the reservoir in which they form before migration reshuffles them. A disk caught in the act of becoming such a reservoir, with its inner structure already organised, connects the statistical abundance of these planets to a concrete, observed formation channel.

The paper is observational rather than definitive, and the interpretation carries the usual caveats of working at the edge of a single object. One young system, however well observed, is a data point and a hypothesis generator, not a rule, and the team's reading of the disk's evolution will be tested against more targets and more models. But the direction is consequential: if the most common planets form through the channel this disk represents, then the frequency of sub-Neptunes in the Galaxy is less mysterious than it appeared, a natural outcome of an ordinary process rather than a quirk of statistics.

There is a pleasing inversion in the result. The planets we can study best, the giant worlds and the rocky ones, are the rarer kinds, while the dominant class hides from detailed scrutiny. A disk that promises to show the dominant class being made is worth more than another well-studied giant: it is a window onto the majority of the Galaxy's real estate, the worlds that outnumber our own kind of planet a hundred to one and about which, until recently, we had only statistics and guesses.
