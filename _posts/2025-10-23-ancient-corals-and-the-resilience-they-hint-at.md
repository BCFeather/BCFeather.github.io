The story of coral reefs in the age of warming has been, for the most part, a story of loss: bleaching events, dying frameworks, shrinking buffers, the repeated announcement that the reefs are being pushed past their limits. A paper in the 23 October issue of *Nature* adds a complicating chapter to that story. Some ancient stony corals, the researchers report, survived extreme environmental changes in the geological past, and the record of their survival raises the possibility that some modern species retain a measure of the resilience that their ancestors displayed. The finding does not soften the picture of reef decline; it sharpens it, by asking which corals carry the capacity to endure, and under what conditions.

The evidence comes from the deep past. The geological record preserves periods of environmental upheaval, of warming, acidification and shifting sea levels, that were as severe as anything the modern reefs face, and the fossil record shows that some stony coral lineages persisted through them. The paper reconstructs which groups survived and which were lost, and the pattern of survival is the study's core finding: resilience, the record suggests, was not spread evenly across the corals, but concentrated in certain groups with particular characteristics.

The relevance to the present follows from that pattern. If the capacity to survive environmental extremes is a heritable property, unevenly distributed, then the modern reefs' fate is not uniform: some species may harbour the resilience that carried their ancestors through past upheavals, while others, including many of the framework-builders that today's reefs depend on, may not. The paper is careful not to promise that ancient endurance translates automatically into modern survival, but it identifies the species in which the capacity might be found, and where the search for it should concentrate. The distinction matters for the reefs' future management: a reef's prospects depend not only on how much the ocean warms but on which of its species carry the traits that the past has already vetted.

The scientific value of the work is in the way it reframes the question. The reefs' vulnerability has been established beyond serious doubt; the interesting question now is variation within that vulnerability, which corals are robust, which are fragile, and what the difference depends on. An account of resilience, mapped onto the living species, gives conservation and restoration a target: the traits that let corals endure, and the lineages that carry them.

The deep past, in this reading, becomes a resource for the present. The corals that outlasted the ancient upheavals left a record of what endurance requires, and the record is now being read against the living reefs. The paper's message is not that the reefs will be fine; it is that they will not all fail together, that the capacity to survive is real, unevenly held, and worth studying precisely because the coming pressure will test it. The corals that survived before are gone, but their resilience may survive, in the lineages that carry it, and the study has begun the work of finding them.
