On 10 September the country observes Teachers' Day, one of the few annual occasions dedicated to a profession rather than an event. The day produces the expected gestures: the flowers at the gate, the cards on the desk, the speeches about the noble calling. All of it is well meant, and all of it, as every teacher knows, is a compressed symbol of something that cannot be symbolised: the actual work, the years of lessons and corrections and conversations that the ceremony condenses into a single morning. The day is for the profession; the work it honours is done, as it always has been, in the unphotographed hours.

The work itself resists description because it is not one work but many. The teacher is simultaneously an instructor, explaining material that some students will grasp quickly and others will need to be told again, and again; an administrator, managing the machinery of homework, marks, attendance and the hundred small transactions that fill a classroom; and something rarer, a witness, present in the lives of the students across months and years, noticing the quiet change, the improvement that no test will ever capture. The job is intellectually demanding, emotionally exposed and structurally underappreciated, and it is done, in the main, by people who chose it knowing all of this.

The economics of the profession are a perennial conversation and not a cheerful one. The gap between the social importance of teaching and its material rewards is one of the most durable features of the modern labour market, in this country and others, and the annual ceremony does not close it. What is less often said is that the gap is itself a form of information: it tells us, bluntly, what a society is willing to pay for the thing it claims to value most, and the classroom is one of the few places where the contradiction between stated values and actual priorities is measurable every month.

And yet the institution endures, which is worth reflecting on. The classroom is one of the least automated institutions in modern life: the century that replaced the typewriter and the ledger and the filing clerk has left the basic unit of education, one adult, a room, thirty children, recognisably unchanged. The persistence is not conservatism; it is evidence. The work the teacher does, the attention that no screen can distribute, the judgement that no algorithm can delegate, has turned out to be genuinely hard to replace, and every attempt to automate it has discovered that the teacher's real function was not the delivery of information but something else entirely.

The day will pass, the flowers will wilt, and the teachers will be back in the classrooms, as they were the day before and will be the day after. The honest tribute to the profession is not the ceremony but the recognition of what the ceremony cannot contain: the years of steady, exacting, mostly uncelebrated attention that make a classroom work. Teachers' Day is a small day on the calendar. The work it marks is measured in decades, and it is, by the evidence of every generation that passes through it, the work that no machine has yet been able to do.
