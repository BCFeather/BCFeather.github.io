---

The deep-sea hydrothermal vent is one of the most remote environments on Earth. It sits two or more kilometres below the surface, in darkness, fed by chemical energy rather than sunlight, home to snails and mussels that live around plumes of superheated water. Until recently, it was assumed to be insulated from the plastic mess of the surface. A study published on 3 June in *Water Research*, and announced in detail on 15 July, removes that assumption. Animals collected from vents in the North Fiji Basin and the Central Indian Ridge contained microplastics in 92 per cent of individuals examined, at an average of 3.42 particles per animal.

The work, led by Se-Joo Kim and Jinyoung Jeong at the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology, with Korea's ocean research institute, is the first cross-ocean comparison of plastic contamination in vent fauna. The team compared grazing snails and filter-feeding mussels from two very different basins. The result is not just that the particles are there, but how they got there and where they sit inside the animals.

Feeding style decided the distribution. In grazing snails, which crop microbial mats off the rocks, the microplastics were concentrated in the digestive organs. In filter-feeding mussels, which sieve particles out of the water, the particles were spread more evenly through the tissues. That is a useful result for monitoring, because it means the same animal can be sampled to answer different questions about how plastics move through a deep food web.

The bigger surprise was the difference between oceans. After normalizing for body weight, the Indian Ocean animals carried up to 14.7 times the microplastic load of their south-western Pacific counterparts. The researchers attribute the gap to the surrounding human activity, riverine plastic inputs and the large-scale circulation that delivers surface plastic down into the deep. Polystyrene, the packaging plastic, was the most common polymer found.

The framing of the study is worth a moment. Hydrothermal vents are not just remote ecosystems; they are also the sites of growing interest for deep-sea mining, which would chew up the seafloor around the vents for metals. Showing that these animals are already contaminated with surface plastic, and that the load varies systematically between ocean basins, gives regulators a baseline against which future mining impact can be measured. The paper's authors explicitly make that case: before any deep-sea extraction begins, there is now a record of what the vent fauna looked like before it did.

The broader point is the one the paper leads with. An estimated 11 million tonnes of plastic enter the oceans each year, and the assumption that the deep sea, which is nearly 90 per cent of the marine environment, is somehow beyond the reach of that plastic was always a hope rather than a finding. This study shows the particles are being carried thousands of metres down, deposited in the food web of the most isolated habitats, and accumulating at rates that track surface human activity. There is no deep-sea equivalent of a remote lake.

It is also a reminder that plastic pollution does its work quietly, at the level of individual particles inside individual snails, rather than in the form of an island of bottles on the surface. The dramatic images are the ones that get the attention. The 92 per cent figure is the quieter result, and the one that is harder to argue with. The vents, it turns out, were never insulated. They were only unmeasured.
