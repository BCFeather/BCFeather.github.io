The European rearmament that the year's security crisis forced into the open moved into its concrete phase in the middle of June, and the concreteness was the part that the coverage could finally write about in the language of the numbers. The member states' defence budgets have arrived through the spring's parliamentary sessions, and the increases, the percentages that the governments have committed to, are now being converted into the procurement lists: the tanks, the artillery, the air defences, the munitions that the war on the continent's edge has shown the continent lacks. The rearmament's arithmetic is the story that the budgets are beginning to tell, and the telling is the quiet work that the year's security politics has made urgent.

The budgets' shape is the part that the defence economists have been analysing since the commitments were made. The largest members have committed to the increases that take their spending toward the level that the alliance has long requested, the smaller members have followed with the increases that their economies can carry, and the collective figures, published by the alliance's headquarters, have moved toward the target that the years of under-spending had made distant; the arithmetic is real, and the reality is the consequence of the war and the American pressure, the two forces that the year has combined. The budgets are the beginning, and the beginning's scale will be tested by the procurement that follows.

The procurement lists are the part where the rearmament's difficulties become visible. The continent's defence industries, run down through the decades of under-investment, cannot produce the volumes that the lists demand at the speed that the urgency requires, and the production lines, the ammunition plants, the shipyards, are being expanded with the particular difficulty that the industrial base's condition imposes; the orders that the lists contain will be filled over the years, and the years are the timeline that the security environment cannot guarantee. The procurement's economics, the competition for the contracts, the coordination that the joint orders require, are the subject of the negotiations that the summer's defence meetings will conduct.

The coordination question is the one that the rearmament's arithmetic cannot answer alone. The member states are rearming in parallel, with the national priorities shaping the lists, and the parallel programmes carry the duplication that the economists have long identified: the separate tank programmes, the separate artillery, the separate air-defence systems, each developed at the national scale and none at the scale that the continent's defence would require. The joint procurement initiatives that the war has revived are the attempt to answer the coordination question, and the attempt's progress, through the summer's meetings, will determine whether the rearmament is the sum of the national programmes or the beginning of the continental one.

The rearmament's politics are the part that the budgets will test through the year. The public support that the war generated has carried the increases through the parliaments, and the support's durability will be tested by the trade-offs that the spending produces, the welfare programmes, the taxes, the debt; the governments that approved the budgets will have to defend the trade-offs through the coming electoral cycles, and the defence of the trade-offs will be the rearmament's political story. The defence budgets arrived in the summer of 2025 with the procurement lists that the war had made necessary, and the lists' fulfilment will be the work of the decade; the arithmetic is being set, and the arithmetic's consequences, the production, the coordination, the politics, will be the story that the budgets have begun.
