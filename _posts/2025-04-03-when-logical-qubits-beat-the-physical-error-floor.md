---

A quantum computer that runs more than a hundred million operations fault-tolerantly must suppress its errors down to levels that shrink as the machine grows. That is the whole promise of quantum error correction, and for years the catch was that encoding a qubit made it more error-prone, not less. The overhead of the syndrome measurements ate the gains. A preprint posted to arXiv on 2 April 2024 reports that this break-even line has now been crossed on a trapped-ion processor. 'Demonstration of logical qubits and repeated error correction with better-than-physical error rates', by A. Paetznick and colleagues at Microsoft Azure Quantum and Quantinuum, runs the correction loop well enough that the protected qubit outperforms the unprotected one.

The processor is Quantinuum's H-series trapped-ion system, a quantum charge-coupled device in which individual ions are shuttled between zones for gates and readout. The team uses two codes. The first is the [[7,1,3]] Steane code, the canonical seven-physical-qubit encoding of a single logical qubit. The second is a [[12,2,4]] code derived from Knill's C4/C6 scheme, which packs two logical qubits into twelve physical ones. These are small codes by the standards of the eventual surface-code machines, but they are the ones whose gate-level overhead a high-fidelity ion system can actually run.

The numbers are the point. Entangling two Steane-encoded logical qubits produced error rates between 9.8 and 500 times lower than the same operation on the underlying physical qubits, depending on the configuration. The C4/C6 code did even better, with entangled-logical error rates 4.7 to 800 times below the physical baseline. Those ranges are wide because they straddle the choice of post-selection, a caveat the authors are careful to flag. The more convincing result is the repeated-correction experiment on the twelve-qubit code, where the logical error rate stayed below the physical-circuit baseline that a chain of uncorrected CNOT gates would accumulate.

The cleanest single metric is the cost of one correction cycle. Each cycle performs more than a hundred physical CNOT gates, yet the error it adds approaches the error of just two physical CNOTs. In other words, the protection is almost free: the machinery of detection and syndrome extraction introduces barely more error than the gates it protects. That is the property a fault-tolerant architecture needs, because it means stacking correction rounds does not come with a tax that erodes the very fault tolerance it buys.

What makes this distinct from the better-known superconducting results is the platform and the regime. This is not a thousand-qubit surface code with ever-larger logical patches. It is a small, high-fidelity ion system demonstrating that the full loop, encoding, repeated syndrome measurement, mid-circuit correction and a two-logical-qubit entangling gate, can be closed in real time with a net error reduction. The authors frame it as a transition from noisy intermediate-scale hardware to reliable quantum computing, a phrase that invites scepticism but is supported by the break-even data.

The honest qualifications are that the gains lean on post-selection in parts of the analysis, that the codes are short by the standards of error correction at scale, and that the path from a dozen physical qubits to a million is a long one. No one is claiming a useful calculation here. What the paper establishes is narrower and more useful: the correction loop itself is no longer the thing that makes a logical qubit worse. Once that loop breaks even, the question becomes purely about how quickly it improves as the physical gates get cleaner, and the trapped-ion numbers suggest the answer is encouraging.
