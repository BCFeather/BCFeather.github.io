There comes a morning in September when the air on the commute smells different, and for a day or two you cannot place what has changed. It is not cold, and it is not yet cool in any decided way. The light is the same, the traffic is the same, the people waiting at the crossing are the same. And then, on the third or fourth pass under the same stretch of trees, it clicks: the osmanthus is out.

Nobody plants osmanthus for the show of it. The trees are ordinary-looking, small-leaved, unassuming, the kind of street tree one walks past for a year without registering a single feature. The flowers are tiny, scattered along the branches like flecks of gold, easy to miss if one is looking at the ground. The only thing they do, and they do it completely, is smell. For perhaps two or three weeks the whole neighbourhood is perfumed with a sweet, honeyed scent that hangs in the cool air and does not blow away. It is the smell of that specific pocket of autumn, and it is impossible to mistake for anything else.

The pleasure is in how little it asks. There is no festival attached, no ticket, no appointed place to stand and admire. The osmanthus simply happens, on the sidewalk, outside the convenience store, over the wall of the small park where the elderly do their morning exercises. One person notices it on the way to work and mentions it over a desk. Another catches it at the bus stop and stands there a moment longer than necessary. Nobody photographs it with any seriousness, because the perfume does not translate into a picture. It is the kind of beauty that is only fully itself in motion, passing through, on the way to somewhere else.

This is the particular genius of a season that announces itself through scent rather than spectacle. Autumn in a city is usually announced by effort: the leaves have to turn, which takes a hard frost, and the sky has to clear, which takes a wind. The osmanthus does not wait for any of that. It simply appears one mild September morning, and for the next fortnight the city is gently, continuously reminded that the year has turned. People who could not name the tree will still say, this week, that the air smells like autumn.

There is something modest about the whole affair that suits the season. The grander displays of the botanical world demand attention and give it back in equal measure. The osmanthus asks for nothing. It does not even demand to be looked at. It only asks to be breathed, and then it is gone, all at once, as quietly as it arrived. One morning the scent is simply not there, and the sidewalk is just a sidewalk again, and the city has moved on without remarking the departure.

That is how these short seasons work. They arrive without ceremony, are lived inside almost without noticing, and are remembered afterwards in a single, sharp association of smell. The ride to work will be replayed in memory, years later, as the time when the air smelled of honey. The osmanthus will not be thanked. It does not need to be. It has already done what it does, for anyone who was paying attention on the morning ride.
