On 18 March a team at the University of Science and Technology of China reported something the quantum computing field has promised for three decades: a logical qubit whose error rate is lower than the error rates of the physical qubits that make it up. The result, published with the full set of raw measurements, is the first demonstration at scale of the threshold that turns a laboratory curiosity into an engineering programme. It is not a quantum computer that solves useful problems. It is the correction mechanism that such a computer will be built on, and that is a different and more important kind of milestone.

The logic of error correction in quantum machines has always been brutal. Quantum states are fragile, and every operation introduces errors, so the standard answer is to spread one logical qubit across many physical ones, letting the majority vote out the mistakes. The catch is that the voting machinery itself has to be good enough; below a certain quality threshold, adding more qubits adds more errors than it removes, and the whole enterprise collapses under its own weight. For years every serious lab hovered just below that line, which made every announcement a claim about a future that had not arrived.

What changed this week is not a new trick but a new level of control. The Chinese team, working with a superconducting architecture, ran a distance-seven surface code across dozens of physical qubits and showed the logical error rate dropping as the code grew, which is the signature behaviour of a system that has finally crossed the threshold. The same month, two other groups published supporting results on different hardware, one in the United States and one in Europe, which suggests that this is not a single lucky experiment but a plateau that several architectures have reached. When different camps arrive at the same wall at the same time, the wall is usually real.

I have spent years rolling my eyes at quantum computing press releases, and I want to keep some of that scepticism. A logical qubit that beats its parts is still a long way from a machine that factors large numbers or simulates a useful chemistry problem; the gap is measured in orders of magnitude, not in press cycles. The honest framing is that this removes the first and hardest uncertainty of the field, the one about whether the correction threshold was reachable at all, and replaces it with a set of engineering questions that are expensive, slow and boring. Boring is good. Boring means the physics is settled enough to argue about the plumbing.

My own view is that the next few years will be defined by scale, not by breakthroughs: more qubits, better control, lower crosstalk, and the slow march of the logical qubit from dozens of physical parts toward thousands. The companies that survive will be the ones that treat error correction as a production problem rather than a research poster. The threshold has been crossed, the era of announcements about thresholds should end, and the era of counting what can actually be computed can begin. It will not be glamorous, and for the first time that feels like genuine progress.
