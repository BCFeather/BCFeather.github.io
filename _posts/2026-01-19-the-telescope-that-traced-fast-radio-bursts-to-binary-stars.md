Fast radio bursts have been one of the least explained phenomena in astronomy since their discovery in 2007. A millisecond of radio energy, brighter than an entire galaxy, arriving from somewhere beyond the Milky Way, with no obvious source and no warning. Over the years the catalogue has grown to thousands, and the theories about what makes them have multiplied at a similar rate. A paper published in Science on 16 January moves the argument forward with the sharpest evidence yet that at least some bursts come from binary systems.

The instrument that made the measurement is the Five-hundred-metre Aperture Spherical radio Telescope, FAST, in Guizhou. A team led by the Purple Mountain Observatory in Nanjing caught a repeating burst in the middle of a dramatic event: the Faraday rotation of its signal jumped sharply and then settled back down. Faraday rotation is a measure of how much a radio signal has been twisted by magnetic fields and charged particles along its path. A sudden jump and recovery means the burst passed through a compact, magnetized environment that changed quickly, the kind of environment you would expect near a companion star orbiting closely around a magnetar or another compact object.

The importance of the measurement is that it connects the burst to a place. The leading family of explanations for repeating bursts involves a highly magnetized neutron star, a magnetar, and its surroundings. The new observation supports the branch of that family in which the magnetar is not alone, but has a companion, often a white dwarf or a stellar remnant, whose wind or debris field injects the material that the radio signal must cross. The burst environment becomes, in effect, a telescope on the binary's orbit, changing its rotation measure as the system moves.

This is the kind of result that observational astronomy accumulates slowly. The burst was caught because FAST can stare at the same patch of sky repeatedly with sensitivity no other instrument reaches, and because the source is one of the few known repeaters bright enough to be studied this closely. The team's paper reports the rotation measure evolving over the course of several observations, a measurement that took years of accumulated data and a patient search for the right source. That patience is worth remarking on. The headline instruments of astronomy tend to be the ones that find things, but the equally valuable work is often the kind that returns to the same object again and again until it does something revealing, which is exactly how most of the discipline's puzzles have been solved.

The wider significance is for the future of FRB science. The field has been waiting for a consensus mechanism, and the evidence has been pointing in different directions: some bursts look like they come from isolated magnetars, others from binaries, and a growing number of studies suggest the two may not be cleanly separable. The FAST result does not settle the whole question, but it demonstrates a technique that can. Rotation-measure monitoring of repeaters, measuring how the environment changes over time, is becoming a standard method, and the telescopes that can do it, FAST included, are now the ones driving the field.

The other half of the story is institutional. FAST spent its early years as a project criticized for building a single-purpose instrument in an era of array telescopes. The FRB work is the reply: the telescope's collecting area, exactly the property the critics worried about, is what makes these measurements possible, and China's radio astronomy has used it to go from observing the field to leading it. The burst that flickered on 16 January is a data point in a larger pattern, one where the instrument, the method and the community have all arrived at the same moment. The next repeater that does something strange will be watched by a much larger network of telescopes, and the binary hypothesis now has a benchmark against which the next observation will be judged.
