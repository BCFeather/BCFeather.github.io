There is a stretch of the calendar, roughly the middle of spring, when a certain sound dominates the streets and offices of the northern cities: the sneeze. The pollen season is a fixed feature of the year, as reliable as the equinox that opens it, and for a significant minority of the population it is the most anticipated and dreaded event of the spring. The runny nose, the itchy eyes, the exhausted concentration, the box of tissues on every desk: hay fever is trivial, in the medical sense, and it is not trivial at all in the lived sense, because it makes the most beautiful weeks of the year the most miserable for millions of people.

The biology is elegant and cruel. Pollen is not trying to harm anyone; it is trying to reproduce, and the immune system, in a subset of people, mistakes the harmless protein for a threat and mounts a full defence. The cascade that follows, histamine, inflammation, the streaming eyes, is an overreaction to a false alarm, and the allergy season is, in effect, an immune system repeatedly misreading the arrival of spring as an invasion. The same trees and grasses that provide the season's green are, for allergic people, the source of weeks of discomfort, and the calendar of the allergies is the calendar of the plants written in reverse.

The interesting thing about the modern allergy season is that it is not what it used to be. The season has lengthened, in many regions, and the pollen counts have climbed, and the change is traceable to familiar forces: rising temperatures extend the flowering period, and higher carbon dioxide levels, which plants use as fertiliser, push them to produce more pollen. The seasonal annoyance has become a small but measurable climate indicator, one that arrives with red eyes rather than spreadsheets. Meanwhile the allergic population itself has grown, for reasons that are debated, with theories pointing at urban living, altered microbiomes, and the general cleanliness of modern childhood.

There is a deeper irony in the geography of the allergy. The worst symptoms often occur in cities, where the pollen is not actually most abundant; the urban environment, with its heat islands and hard surfaces, tends to hold pollen in suspension longer, and the trees that line the avenues are often the very species, planted for shade and beauty, that produce the most allergenic pollen. The city plants its own allergy season, year after year, for the sake of its own greenery. The sneeze, in this reading, is not a quirk of spring but a feature of the built environment, an invoice for the trees we chose.

The remedies are as imperfect as the condition: antihistamines, avoidance, masks on high-count days, immunotherapy for the committed. None of it is a cure, and the season, like the weather, will do what it does. But the hay-fever months are worth a moment of reflection for what they reveal. The allergy is a meeting point of biology, climate and urban design, a place where the body, the atmosphere and the city all have their say, and where the saying is done in sneezes. Spring is beautiful, and it is also, for a large fraction of humanity, a monthly struggle, and both things are true at once.
