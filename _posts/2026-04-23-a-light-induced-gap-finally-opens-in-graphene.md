For more than a decade, graphene has been the proving ground for a beguiling idea: that a beam of light, shone at the right frequency, can rewrite the electronic band structure of a material on the fly, turning a metal into something else, momentarily, while the light is on. The idea is called Floquet engineering, and graphene, with its clean and simple electronic structure, was always the model system for it. The trouble was that the signature of success, a gap opened where the bands cross, had never actually been seen. A paper in *Nature Materials* on 22 March, from Shuyun Zhou's group at Tsinghua, reports that gap at last.

The physics is worth restating. In a solid, electrons move through a periodic lattice, and the lattice's periodic potential opens energy gaps that decide whether the material is a metal, a semiconductor or an insulator. Extend that idea into time: a periodic light field can itself dress the electrons, forming what are called Floquet-Bloch states, and at the crossing points of those states the light can open a hybrid gap. Open such a gap in graphene, and theory predicts a whole wardrobe of exotic effects, including topological states and an anomalous Hall effect that no permanent material can offer.

Why the gap had proved so elusive is itself the story. Graphene is simple, but real measurements are not. Electron dissipation and many-body interactions smear the signal, and a decade of effort had produced progress in related phenomena but never the direct spectroscopic fingerprint, the avoided crossing under a light pump, that would prove the effect was real. Whether Floquet control could actually be achieved in this model material was, by 2026, an open question.

The Tsinghua team's answer came from a home-built experiment. Using an ultrafast time-resolved angle-resolved photoemission system, with a strong-field mid-infrared laser as the pump, they drove the graphene hard enough to see the bands respond. At the crossing point, a gap opened. Crucially, it behaved exactly as the theory said it should: it tracked the pump intensity, the photon energy and the exposure time, and it was anisotropic, vanishing along the direction of the light's polarization and peaking at right angles to it, splitting into two Dirac nodes. That pattern is the fingerprint, the one that distinguishes a genuine light-induced gap from some unrelated artefact.

What the result unlocks is the field. For years the programme of Floquet engineering in graphene has been, in effect, waiting on a demonstration that the central effect exists. Now that it does, the practical claims become testable: light-controlled topology, non-equilibrium superconducting states, electronic switches run by a beam rather than a wire. The accompanying commentary in the same journal called it the resolution of a long-sought problem and a practical recipe for the rest of the field.

The honest limits are that the gap is a transient, created only while the intense light is on, and that moving from a spectroscopic observation to a working device is another project. But after ten years of a beautiful idea that would not quite show itself, the direct evidence is the thing itself. The band structure of graphene, it turns out, really can be rewritten by light.
