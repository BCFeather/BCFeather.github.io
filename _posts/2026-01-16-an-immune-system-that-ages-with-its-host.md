There is a quiet assumption buried in the way medicine treats infection: that the immune system's job is to clear the pathogen, and that a stronger response is a better one. The assumption has been useful, and it has built the field, but a paper in the 15 January issue of *Nature* adds weight to the growing counter-thesis that the body's defences have a second, equally important task. Beyond eliminating the invader, the host must tolerate its own response, and the study shows in mice that the balance between those two tasks is not fixed. It changes with age, in ways that can make the very mechanisms that protect a young animal actively harm an old one.

The study, a mouse model of infection, found that processes which help younger animals survive are, in older animals, part of the problem. The immune response that controls the pathogen in youth generates collateral damage in age, and the balance tips so far that the older host is better served by damping the response than by intensifying it. The finding is a concrete demonstration of an idea, disease tolerance, that immunologists have been developing for years: survival from infection is determined not only by how many microbes the immune system kills, but by how much damage the killing does to the tissues of the host. Two animals with identical pathogen loads can have opposite outcomes if their tolerance differs.

The age dependence is the new and consequential part. If the optimal immune strategy changes across the lifespan, then the one-size-fits-all logic of much of clinical practice looks different. A treatment that accelerates pathogen clearance may be right for a young patient and wrong for an old one, not because the pathogen is different but because the host's capacity to absorb the immune response has changed. The paper is careful to note that the work is in mice, and that whether the same shift operates in humans remains to be confirmed, but the direction of the finding is consistent with a long-standing clinical intuition: infections that a young person shrugs off can be life-threatening in the elderly, and the difference may be as much about tolerance as about immunity.

The larger implication reaches into how clinical trials are designed and how drugs are dosed. Trials routinely report outcomes by age band, but the biological variable behind those bands, the changing tolerance of the host, is rarely treated as something that could be measured and targeted. If tolerance can be modulated, a whole class of interventions opens up: instead of only helping the immune system fight harder, medicine could help the host endure the fight, protecting tissue from the collateral damage of the response. That is a different kind of drug, aimed at a different target, and it would matter most in exactly the population where fighting harder currently does the most harm.

The study does not claim to have solved ageing or to have found the switch for tolerance. It demonstrates a mechanism in one model system, and it joins a body of work that is slowly redrawing the map of what immunity is for. The immune system, in this view, is not simply a weapon. It is a regulator of damage, balancing clearance against cost, and the balance is set differently at different ages. Medicine built on clearance alone was always missing half the equation; the missing half is now starting to be measured.
