---

On 11 June, the Governing Council of the European Central Bank did something it had not done since September 2023: it raised interest rates. All three key rates went up by a quarter of a percentage point, taking the deposit facility to 2.25 per cent, the main refinancing operations to 2.40 per cent and the marginal lending facility to 2.65 per cent, with the increase taking effect from 17 June. The decision came after years of easing, and the stated reason was not an overheating domestic economy but an external shock: the war in the Middle East was generating inflation pressures, and the bank wanted to be sure those pressures did not harden into expectations.

The shape of the move is the story. This is not a central bank slamming on the brakes because wages and prices are running away. It is a quarter-point, pre-emptive step taken to anchor expectations, the kind of move a bank makes when it worries that a temporary energy-driven price rise could turn into a wage-price spiral if people start to expect it. The accompanying statement was careful to say the decision was robust across a range of scenarios for how the shock might unfold, which is central-bank language for: we are not sure how bad this gets, but we want to be ahead of it.

The backdrop is awkward. Europe entered the conflict with inflation already uncomfortably close to target and growth already weak. The bank simultaneously cut its 2026 growth forecast to 0.8 per cent, which is a meagre figure for a region that needs faster incomes to service its debt. Raising rates into a slowdown is never comfortable, because it adds to the pressure on households with mortgages and on governments carrying large deficits. The bet is that doing nothing would be worse: letting inflation expectations de-anchor would force a much uglier correction later.

The divergence from the transatlantic side of the Atlantic is striking. Across the water, the Federal Reserve has been holding steady and signalling patience, judging that its own economy is expanding at a solid pace. Europe, by contrast, is tightening while growing slowly, because its exposure to the energy shock is larger and its starting inflation position less forgiving. The result is a transatlantic rate gap moving the wrong way for European savers and the right way, for a while, for anyone betting on the euro.

What makes the move risky is the uncertainty about the shock itself. If the conflict stays contained and energy prices settle, a quarter-point hike looks like over-tightening in a fragile economy. If energy costs keep climbing, the same quarter point will look like a first step on a longer path. The bank has, in effect, bought itself option value, and paid for it in slower growth now.

For households and businesses, the practical effect is gradual. Borrowing costs at the margin edge higher, mortgage renewals become a little more expensive, and the incentive to delay spending strengthens. The bank is betting that a little of that discomfort now is preferable to a much larger dose later. Whether the gamble pays off depends on a war whose course no central bank controls, which is the particular discomfort of tightening in response to something outside one's own doors.
