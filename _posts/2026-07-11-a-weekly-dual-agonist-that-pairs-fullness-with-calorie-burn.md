Obesity medicine is no longer a single-agonist story. The first wave of GLP-1 drugs worked by slowing the stomach, blunting appetite and making patients feel full on less food. They worked, and they worked better than anything before them, but they worked one mechanism at a time. The next generation of drugs asks why not add a second mechanism, one that raises energy expenditure and treats the liver rather than only the stomach. Survodutide, a once-weekly injectable from Boehringer Ingelheim, is the most advanced test of that idea. Its phase 3 trial, SYNCHRONIZE-1, was published in *the New England Journal of Medicine* on 7 June 2026.

The biology is the pitch. GLP-1 is the hormone that tells the brain the stomach is full; activating its receptor suppresses appetite. Glucagon is the hormone that raises blood sugar and, at controlled doses, increases energy expenditure and reduces liver fat. Pairing the two on a single molecule is hard, because glucagon agonism at high doses is messy, but if the ratio is right the result is a drug that both makes people want to eat less and burns more of what they do eat. The trial enrolled adults with obesity or overweight but without type 2 diabetes, randomized them to weekly survodutide or placebo for 76 weeks, and measured weight loss.

The headline numbers, on the prespecified efficacy estimand, are large. Participants on survodutide lost an average of 16.6 per cent of their body weight at 76 weeks, against 3.2 per cent on placebo, a difference the authors describe as highly significant. The secondary endpoints moved in the same direction: waist circumference shrank, blood pressure and triglycerides improved, and imaging subsets showed reductions in visceral and liver fat. A substantial proportion of participants crossed the more ambitious thresholds, with many losing at least 15 per cent and a meaningful subgroup reaching 20 per cent or more.

That is the good news. The harder questions are about how the result compares and what it means. On the treatment-policy estimand, which counts everyone who entered the trial whether or not they finished it, the absolute loss is smaller, around the 12-to-13 per cent range that commentators have flagged. The placebo arm itself lost several per cent, which is high by historical standards and pulls the between-group gap down. There was no active comparator against tirzepatide or semaglutide, so the trial cannot say whether survodutide is better than the best existing option, only that it works on its own terms.

The tolerability profile is the familiar one. Gastrointestinal side effects were the reason most often cited for discontinuation, and a drug that people stop taking after a year is a drug that cannot keep its patients on the scale. The cardiovascular outcomes data that really decides the future of this class, the kind that showed semaglutide reducing major heart events, are not yet in for survodutide. Until they are, the drug is a weight-loss treatment, not yet a heart-outcome treatment.

What the trial establishes, though, is the principle. Dual receptor agonism survived a phase 3 readout. The glucagon arm of the molecule did not turn out to be the dosing disaster some had feared, and the liver-fat and expenditure signals held up at scale. That is the result the field was waiting for, and it opens the door to the next generation of molecules that combine appetite suppression with metabolic activation rather than trying to do everything through the stomach.

The trial is not the end of the argument. It is the proof that the dual-agonist approach is a real category rather than a curiosity. The next question is whether the extra mechanism buys anything an existing GLP-1 does not, and the head-to-head and outcomes trials that will answer that are already running. For now, survodutide is the strongest evidence yet that the obesity drug cupboard is not closed.
