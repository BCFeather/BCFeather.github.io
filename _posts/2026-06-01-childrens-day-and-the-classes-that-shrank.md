Children's day in China arrived this year with an unusual piece of good news buried in the school statistics, and the good news is the kind that the headlines usually miss: the average primary class size has fallen below forty for the first time in two decades. The number is the arithmetic consequence of a decade of declining births meeting a school system built for a larger generation, and the arithmetic is now working in the teachers' favour. The smaller classes are a demographic dividend that nobody planned for, and the education system is only beginning to decide what to do with it.

The scale of the change is worth being precise about. The peak cohorts of the 2010s filled the primary schools with classes of fifty and above in the big cities, and the teachers who managed those rooms developed the pedagogy that such rooms require: lecture-heavy, discipline-first, with little room for the individual attention that the smaller classes now make possible. The current cohorts, the children born in the early 2020s, have been entering school as the numbers were already falling, and the classrooms that once squeezed fifty desks now hold thirty-five. The change is not uniform, the cities still crowd and the countryside empties, but the average has crossed a line that the profession has been asking for since the century turned.

The interesting question is what the system does with the room it has been given. The official response so far has been cautious, and the caution is understandable: the smaller classes have arrived at the same time as the school-age population is shrinking, and the policy makers are worried less about the class size than about the empty buildings and the teacher surplus that will follow the smaller cohorts through the system. The districts are responding by merging schools, retiring the older teachers early, and redesigning the teacher training quotas around a smaller future, which are the sensible adjustments of an institution facing a smaller clientele. The risk is that the adjustments are made in the language of efficiency rather than the language of quality, and that the smaller classes are treated as a problem to be managed rather than an opportunity to be used.

The falling numbers are the biggest educational opportunity in a generation, and the schools that grasp it will look very different from the ones that merely shrink. A class of thirty-five, taught by a teacher with no incentive to fill the desks, can be taught the way the educational research has always said teaching works best: more discussion, more individual feedback, more time for the students who need it. The districts that treat the surplus teachers as a chance to cut the ratios rather than the payroll, that invest the savings in the smaller classes rather than returning them to the budget, will be the ones that the students remember. The money is there, the teachers are there, and the room is now there, which is a combination that the system has not had since the last generation's boom began.

The demographic reality behind the numbers will keep asserting itself, and the empty schools and the surplus graduates are real problems that no amount of classroom quality will solve. But the children in the smaller classes this June are being taught in conditions that their older siblings never had, and the system that uses the conditions well will produce a cohort that the efficiency experts may not have anticipated. Children's day is a day for the small pleasures of childhood, and this year it has a statistic to go with it: fewer desks in the room, more of the teacher's attention in reach, and a generation that, for the first time in twenty years, is not being educated in a crowd.
