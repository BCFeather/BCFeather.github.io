The negotiations over the tariff programme entered their second month in mid-May, with the pause's deadline fixed on the calendar and the talks proceeding at the pace that the trade diplomacy has always set. The technical sessions have run through the weeks, the ministerial calls have been conducted, and the statements have been issued and parsed with the care that the year's conflict has taught the markets; the accumulation of the sessions and the statements is the negotiation's progress, such as it is, and the progress is being measured against the deadline that the summer will bring. The pause's end will arrive on a fixed date; the agreement that would make the pause permanent will arrive, if it arrives, on the negotiators' schedule, and the two calendars are the tension of the talks.

The state of the talks, as the second month opened, is the state that the participants describe with the language of the progress and the caveats. The technical work has produced the mapping of the sectors and the rates that the agreements would cover, and the mapping is the necessary foundation that the trade negotiations always build before the politics engage; the political work, the concessions and the conditions, has proceeded more slowly, with the two sides' red lines holding through the sessions and the flexibility limited to the margins. The negotiators' statements, delivered through the spokespeople, have described the talks as constructive, and the description, the analysts note, is the diplomacy's way of saying that the talks are continuing, which is the honest measure of the progress.

The sectors that the talks cover are the part of the story that the industries on both sides are following with the attention that the stakes require. The exporters who have seen the tariffs' costs have pressed for the speed, and the industries that the tariffs were designed to protect have pressed for the terms, and the two pressures have reached the negotiators through the channels that the domestic politics provide; the agreements that the talks are building, the sectoral deals and the carve-outs that the technical sessions have sketched, will be tested by the industries' reception, and the reception will be the political dimension of the settlement. The sectors' arithmetic is the talks' background, and the arithmetic is the pressure that the negotiations operate under.

The deadline's approach has begun to concentrate the negotiators' minds, as the deadlines always do. The pause's end, scheduled for the summer, would restore the full tariff programme that the pause suspended, and the restoration is the threat that the deadline carries; the threat is the leverage that both sides hold, and the leverage is the reason the talks have continued rather than collapsed. The weeks ahead will see the acceleration that the deadlines produce, the intensified sessions, the leaders' calls, the draft agreements circulated and revised, and the acceleration will determine whether the settlement can be reached within the window that the pause provides or whether the deadline will bring the escalation that the year's pattern threatens.

The settlement that the talks are building, if it comes, will be the year's largest economic event, and the settlement's shape will be the shape that the negotiators have been sketching through the spring: the reduction of the rates on the affected goods, the carve-outs for the sensitive sectors, the mechanisms for the monitoring and the disputes. The shape is the outline, and the outline requires the political will that the two sides' governments will have to supply; the summer deadline will test the will, and the testing will be conducted in the full presence of the markets, the industries and the voters that the settlement will affect. The talks hold their course as the deadline approaches, and the course will be held, or changed, by the events of the summer's first weeks.
