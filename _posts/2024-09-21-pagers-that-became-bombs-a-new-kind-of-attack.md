On the afternoon of 17 September, the pagers began to explode. Across Beirut and the south of Lebanon, in markets and shops and homes, the small devices that Hezbollah had distributed to its members as a security measure, the low-technology alternative to mobile phones, detonated in their owners' pockets, first a single beep, then a blast that severed hands and tore through bodies at waist height. The following day, a second wave, this time of walkie-talkies, delivered the same lesson to the funerals of the first. When the two days were over, more than thirty people were dead and close to three thousand wounded, civilians and fighters alike, and the world's understanding of what an attack can be had changed overnight.

The mechanics are the story, and the mechanics are terrifying in their ordinariness. The pagers, purchased by Hezbollah months earlier, had been intercepted somewhere in the supply chain between manufacturer and buyer, fitted with a small explosive charge and a triggering device, and delivered to the organisation's members with the bomb already inside the package. When the signal came, the devices the fighters had carried as protection killed them on contact. The attack was not a strike on a building or a convoy but an operation against a procurement system, and its reach extended to anyone who happened to be carrying a device, a shopkeeper, a bystander, a child. The distinction between combatant and civilian, already fragile in this war, was erased by the logistics of the weapon.

The attribution was never in doubt. Lebanon and Hezbollah blamed Israel within hours, and the Israeli government, in its usual manner, neither confirmed nor denied; American officials later confirmed that Israel had been responsible and had informed Washington in advance. The strategic context made the operation's purpose legible: it came as the war between Israel and Hezbollah was approaching its escalation, and the strikes were a demonstration that no method of theirs was beyond reach. The head of Hezbollah, Hassan Nasrallah, called the attack a crossing of 'all red lines' that amounted to a declaration of war, and the phrase, for once, described the situation accurately from both sides' perspective.

The precedent is the reason the attack will be remembered beyond the war that produced it. Every government, every military, every organisation that buys equipment from abroad has just been shown that the product it trusts can be turned against it, and the demonstration does not require any particular sophistication: the devices were ordinary consumer electronics, the interception happened at the level of distribution, and the explosives were small. The response will be the search for alternatives, the demand for domestic supply chains, the suspicion of anything made abroad, and the suspicion will be rational, which makes it all the more corrosive. The world of trade that the globalised economy built runs on the assumption that goods are what they appear to be; the attack in Lebanon was an argument, delivered in the most direct way possible, that the assumption is optional.

The deeper question, the one that will be argued in the ethics committees and the war colleges for years, is what the attack was. It was, by any definition, an act of war, and it was also an act of terrorism in the sense that it deliberately struck at civilians in public spaces, however precise its intelligence about the devices was. It killed fighters and non-combatants alike, it was carried out without warning, and it transformed a piece of everyday life into an instrument of death, and every one of those properties will be cited, by someone, as either the operation's justification or its condemnation. The arguments will not be settled, and neither will the technology; the devices that explode in pockets have joined the repertoire of conflict, and the next war will have learned the lesson of the pagers. The precedent is set.
