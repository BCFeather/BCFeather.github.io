On the evening of 24 August, Pavel Durov stepped off a private jet from Azerbaijan at Le Bourget airport outside Paris, and was arrested by French police before he could leave the terminal. The founder and chief executive of Telegram, a platform with close to a billion users, had been wanted, according to the French prosecutors who confirmed the detention, for questioning over the use of his messaging app for organised crime: drug trafficking, fraud, the distribution of child sexual abuse material, and, most fundamentally, for refusing, as a matter of company principle, to cooperate with the authorities who came asking. The arrest transformed a technical debate about encryption and moderation into a geopolitical incident, with Moscow denouncing a political persecution and the free-speech wing of the internet rallying to Durov's side.

The details of the French case matter, because they define what is actually being tried. The investigation, opened in February, targets not Durov's personal conduct but the structure of the company itself: the charge that Telegram was used as a tool of crime by people the platform did nothing to stop, and that the founder, by refusing to cooperate with French judicial requests, made himself a participant by omission. The legal theory, sometimes called complicity by abstention, is one that European courts have applied to platforms before, and it is a theory that Telegram, which has built its identity on resisting exactly this kind of pressure, was always going to collide with sooner or later.

The collision was foreseeable, and that is the most uncomfortable part of the affair for both sides. Telegram's promise to its users, encrypted, unmoderated, indifferent to governments, is also a promise to criminals that their channels will be safe, and the platform's own history shows the price of that promise: it has been the communication tool of choice for insurgent groups, scam networks and disinformation operations, and its refusal to build the ordinary moderation machinery that every other large platform maintains has been a standing invitation to the very activity the French are investigating. The libertarian purity of the founder's position is consistent; consistency is not the same as innocence, and the difference between protecting speech and protecting crime is a line that Telegram has declined, on principle, to draw.

The arrest has already produced the reactions that the case was bound to produce. Russia, where Durov built the company before leaving in 2014 rather than comply with the security services, called the detention a political act and demanded consular access; the supporters who gathered outside the courthouse in Paris held signs about free speech; and the platform's defenders, including many who normally disagree with everything Moscow says, found themselves making the same argument from the opposite side of the world. The alignment is the clue to the problem: the case has become a proxy for the oldest argument on the internet, about who is responsible for what flows through a channel that no one owns, and the positions have frozen along the lines of that argument rather than along the evidence.

Whatever the courts decide, the affair has already done its work. Every platform with a billion users and a moderation policy that depends on goodwill has been reminded that the goodwill is revocable; every regulator has been reminded that a single arrest can do what years of negotiation could not; and every user of Telegram, which is to say a substantial fraction of the human internet, has been handed the question that the app's design has always avoided: what is the platform actually for, and who answers for it. The answer that France extracts from Durov, whatever it is, will be written into the terms of the internet for a decade.
