The longest day of the year arrives and nobody marks it. There is no holiday, no ceremony, no moment when the city pauses to acknowledge that the light has peaked and will now, by the smallest of degrees, begin its retreat. You notice it only in hindsight: that evening at dinner, looking up, and realizing that the sky is still blue at seven when it should, by rights, have gone dark.

The solstice is a quiet revolution. For six months the evenings have been drawing out, one or two minutes at a time, until you look up in June and the light has got away from you. The children who came home from school in the dark of January are now playing outside long after supper, and the commuters who left work in the twilight of spring are walking home through a sun that has not, strictly speaking, begun to set. The city has adjusted to this extra hour the way it adjusts to everything, without remark.

What the light does is change the texture of the evening. The usual schedule is built around darkness: the lamps come on, the curtains close, the television goes on, the day is declared over. On these longest evenings the schedule rebels. People linger on their doorsteps longer than they meant to. They take a walk after dinner that turns into two, because the pavement is still bright and the air still warm. The exercise yards fill late, the riverside paths fill late, and the whole city, for a few weeks, conducts its social life outdoors in a way it does not at any other time of year.

The light also reveals what the darker months hid. One sees, in the long evening, the shape of the rooftops, the line of the hills on the horizon, the way the west window fills with a gold that lasts an hour. The city is lit from the outside, rather than by its own sodium lamps, and it looks, briefly, like a different place. The concrete and glass, which the daylight flattens, take on a warmth they will not have again until September.

There is a melancholy tucked into the solstice, if one looks for it. The longest day is also the turning point. From tomorrow the evenings will shorten, by a minute at a time, and the summer, which feels endless in June, has already begun its slow retreat. People sense this without saying it, which is why the longest evenings have a slight urgency: the picnic in the park, the walk along the river, the drink on the terrace, all of it conducted with the quiet knowledge that the season is a countdown.

The solstice itself passes without fanfare, and that is perhaps as it should be. The longest day is not an event; it is a condition. One lives inside it for a few weeks, walking home through light that should be dark, and then, by imperceptible steps, the light goes back. The city does not need to mark the turning. It has lived through it every year for as long as anyone can remember, and it will live through it again.
