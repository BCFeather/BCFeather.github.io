On the evening of 19 July 2026, the final of the first forty-eight-team World Cup ended, appropriately, in the way the whole tournament had felt: Spain won, in extra time, against an opponent that had refused to go quietly. Ferran Torres scored the decisive goal after a run of football that had dominated the month, and Spain lifted the trophy on the same field in Miami where the expanded, three-host tournament had been drawing record crowds all summer. The United States, Canada and Mexico had staged a World Cup that was, by the numbers and the atmosphere, the largest in history.

The tournament was defined by two stories running in parallel. The first was Spain. From the opening rounds the side played the possession football that has been the signature of its generation, and it met the tournament's strongest challenges in the final stages: a France side that had reached the final by outlasting its opponents, and an Argentina led by a veteran Lionel Messi in what was universally described as his last World Cup. The final itself was tense, decided only in extra time, and the margin reflected the difference in control rather than the difference in effort.

The second story was the expansion itself. Forty-eight teams, up from thirty-two, brought in sides from every confederation and produced the inevitably lopsided group matches, but it also produced the upsets and the long-shot runs that make a World Cup memorable. France's march to the final and the bronze-medal match, decided 6-4 against England in Miami, delivered the kind of open, high-scoring spectacle that the format was meant to encourage. Mbappé finished as the tournament's top scorer with ten goals, and the numbers that followed the final confirmed the scale: a tournament watched in more countries, and by more people, than any before it.

The deeper question is what such a tournament means for the game itself. A forty-eight-team format spreads the calendar, stretches the players and tilts the economics toward the biggest markets, and its critics argued throughout that it prizes breadth over the purity of the knockout stages. The counter-argument, which the summer made hard to dismiss, is that the expansion grew the game: it gave smaller federations their moment, drew new audiences, and produced a final between two of the strongest sides the sport has produced, in a stadium packed with the world's attention.

Spain's triumph felt earned in the fullest sense. A side that played the game the way its academy has taught it for a generation, that met every test and conceded little, won the trophy that its football has long promised. The tournament as a whole, for all its length and its sprawl, did what a World Cup is meant to do: it made a claim about the state of the game and the scale of its audience. The game is bigger than it has ever been, and on this evidence it will keep growing, for better and worse.
