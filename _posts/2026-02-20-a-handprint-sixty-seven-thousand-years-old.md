---

On the cover of the 19 February issue of *Nature* there is a hand, a stencil in red pigment, blown or daubed around a hand pressed against a cave wall. Hands are the most universal motif in rock art, found on every inhabited continent, and the most personal: each one is a self-portrait, a signature that says I was here. The team behind the cover image reports that this particular hand, at a site called Liang Metanduno, is dated to at least 67,800 years ago, which would make it the oldest known rock art in the world, and the oldest evidence of a very old question being asked: when did our ancestors first decide to make marks that mean something?

The dating is the crucial and difficult part. Rock art is notoriously hard to date; the pigment is often mineral, the surface is rock, and there is rarely organic material to measure. The team used methods applied to the mineral crusts that formed over and around the art, constraining when the art must already have existed. The result, at least 67,800 years, pushes the stencil beyond previous records by a meaningful margin, and it places the act within a period that older models treated as too early for symbolic behaviour. The paper's caution is appropriate, and independent verification will follow, but the claim is made with enough stratigraphic care to be taken seriously.

The significance is not simply that the date is old; it is what the date does to the story of when we became human in the modern sense. The making of representational art has long been treated as a late development, a by-product of complex language and social life that appeared in the last few tens of thousands of years. A hand stencil from nearly seventy thousand years ago, produced by people whose anatomy was essentially modern, suggests that the impulse to represent, to sign, to leave a mark for future eyes, was present much earlier, and that the late dates of most cave art reflect preservation and discovery rather than the origin of the behaviour.

There is also something touching in the specificity of the image. A hand stencil is the only form of art in which the artist is literally present, the outline of the body pressed against the wall, the pigment blown around it. The person who made it could not see the finished image from where they stood, only feel the shape of their own hand. Seventy thousand years later the hand is legible as a hand, and the act is legible as an intention: to make something that outlasts the moment, to place the body in the record of the place, to say I was here, and here means now. It is the oldest known example of a message addressed to a stranger in a future that did not yet exist.

The site joins a growing catalogue of early art that has been redrawing the timeline, and the pattern in the dates is interesting: the oldest candidates cluster in island Southeast Asia, far from the European caves that long defined the subject. The geography matters as much as the chronology. If the earliest marks are coming from this part of the world, then the story of symbolic origins is not a single story from one continent but a wider, older web, and the hand at Liang Metanduno is one of its oldest known threads.
