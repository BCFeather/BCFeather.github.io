On the night of 3 December 2024, South Korea's president Yoon Suk-yeol declared emergency martial law. He cited the need to root out what he called anti-state forces, and he sent soldiers toward the National Assembly and the election commission. Within hours the legislature voted to lift the decree, and by the next morning Yoon had backed down. What followed was one of the fastest and most consequential falls of a head of state in modern Asia.

The timeline tells the story by itself. Parliament impeached Yoon on 14 December. He became the first sitting president in the country's constitutional history to be arrested, on 15 January 2025. The Constitutional Court removed him from office by an 8–0 vote on 4 April, making him the second president, after Park Geun-hye, to be dismissed by impeachment. The criminal cases then followed: he was sentenced to life imprisonment in February 2026 on charges of leading an insurrection, and separately to seven years for obstructing his own arrest.

Several things about the affair are worth dwelling on. The first is how fast the system responded. Martial law was declared at night and reversed before dawn, because parliament refused to accept it and the public refused to be intimidated. The second is the process itself. Yoon was not removed by a mob or a coup but through impeachment, a constitutional court and criminal trials, each step slow, contested and legal. However unpopular he became, the country went through the machinery rather than around it.

The part that most people outside South Korea may have missed is how close it came to not working. Yoon gambled that the military and the public would accept a power grab dressed up as emergency rule, and he lost within hours. His mistake was to treat the constitution as an inconvenience. In doing so he handed his opponents an open-and-shut case, and he tested whether the country's democratic institutions would hold when the man at the top of them decided to break the rules. They held, and that is the part of the story that stays with me.

Democracies are often described as slow and messy, and this one certainly was. But when the person elected to defend the system tried to dismantle it, parliament, the courts and the streets all pushed back within days, not months. That is not a given in any country, including ones that like to lecture others about stability. The lesson cuts both ways: a president who reaches for emergency powers to keep himself in office is testing the whole society, and the society answered.

There is also a quieter caution. Yoon was a prosecutor who had built a career on locking up his political opponents, and his downfall shows how thin the line is between zeal and overreach. The institutions that removed him were not neutral actors; they had themselves been the targets of his abuse. That they removed him anyway, rather than rallying to the executive, is the most reassuring part of the story, and, given how many countries have failed that test recently, the most useful.
