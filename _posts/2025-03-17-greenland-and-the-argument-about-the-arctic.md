The world's largest island became, in the spring of 2025, the subject of the year's most unusual territorial argument, and the argument had the particular character of the new era's geopolitics: the American administration repeated, in the statements and the interviews, its interest in acquiring Greenland, and the Danish and Greenlandic governments insisted, with the consistency that the situation required, that the island is not for sale. The argument has moved the Arctic into the centre of the transatlantic conversation, and the movement is the real story, because the conversation that the island has provoked is about something larger than the island itself: the new American approach to the alliances, the strategic value of the Arctic, and the limits of the pressure that even the closest allies will accept.

The strategic case for the island, repeated in the American statements, is the part that the coverage has weighed with the seriousness it deserves. Greenland sits on the shortest routes between the two hemispheres, hosts the American base that the Arctic operations depend on, and holds the mineral resources, the rare earths, the uranium, that the new industries require; the case for the American interest is genuine, and the case for the acquisition, the administration's argument runs, is the securing of the interest against the rivals that the Arctic is opening to. The argument's logic is the logic of the great-power competition that the new administration applies to everything, and the logic, applied to an allied territory, is the part that the allies find most difficult to accept.

The Danish and Greenlandic response to the argument has been the study in the limits of the small-state diplomacy. The governments have repeated the constitutional facts, that the island's status is the decision of its own people, that the acquisition is not a matter for negotiation, that the relationship with the United States is important and will remain so; the responses have been delivered with the calm that the diplomacy requires, and the calm has been tested by the repeated statements from Washington. The Greenlandic leadership has made the additional point that the island's future is its own, that the independence movement, real and long-standing, will decide the relationship with Copenhagen before any other power decides the relationship with the island; the point is the reminder that the argument's subject has a voice of its own.

The reaction of the European capitals to the argument has been the part that the analysts have watched for the signs of the new transatlantic era. The statements of support for Denmark have been issued, the visits have been scheduled, and the European institutions have found the language that expresses the concern without the confrontation that the relationship could not absorb; the Europeans, the diplomats note, are managing the argument as the test of the new American doctrine, the test of whether the alliance can withstand the pressure that the new administration applies even to its oldest partners. The outcome of the test will be measured in the months ahead, and the measurement will be conducted in the language of the Arctic, which is the language of the ice and the routes and the resources.

The argument about Greenland will not, in all likelihood, end with the island changing hands; the constitutional obstacles, the political costs, and the Greenlandic people's own preference for their future make the acquisition, in the classical sense, a project without a route. What the argument has already done is to establish the terms on which the new American administration will conduct its relationships with the allies: the interest stated plainly, the pressure applied consistently, and the alliances treated as the bargains that the new doctrine declares them to be. The world's largest island has become the emblem of the year's transatlantic argument, and the argument, whatever happens to the island, will shape the alliance's conversation for the rest of the decade.
