On 9 September, Apple held its annual launch event and announced the iPhone 16, and the most striking thing about the presentation was how little of it was about the phone. The hardware was there, in the usual way: two new chips, larger screens on the Pro models, a camera-control button on the side. But the event's centre of gravity was unmistakably software, specifically Apple Intelligence, the company's suite of generative-AI features, which will arrive as a free update in October and which the presentation treated, without apology, as the reason to buy the new phone at all. It was the first time in years that Apple has sold a phone as a vessel for something else.

The feature set is carefully chosen to be legible. Writing tools that rewrite and summarize text across the system; notification summaries that compress the day's interruptions; a redesigned Siri that draws on personal context and can move between apps; an image playground for generating pictures; and a system-wide integration that lets the software see, in a controlled way, what is on the screen. None of this is revolutionary in the abstract, and most of it has been previewed by competitors, but Apple's version carries the two properties the company has always sold: integration, the sense that the features are native to the device rather than bolted on, and privacy, the promise that processing happens on the device or in Apple's private-cloud servers, where data is not stored or used to train models. The promise matters, because it is the one argument Apple can make that the rest of the industry cannot.

The timing is the story beneath the story. Apple is late to the generative-AI party, by its own standards of punctuality: the competitors shipped their assistants and their chatbots a year and more ago, and the market's early judgement was that Apple had missed the wave. The event was the company's answer, and the answer took the form of a claim about what the wave actually is: not chatbots sitting on top of phones, but intelligence woven into the operating system, which is a game Apple has never lost. The claim is partly marketing, but it is not only marketing, and the difference is visible in the details, the on-device models, the permission prompts, the refusal to ship features before they are ready, that the company treats as its brand.

The harder questions sit beside the launch, mostly unasked. Apple Intelligence will not be available, at launch, in the European Union or in China, and the reasons, the DMA's interoperability demands on the one hand and the regulatory requirements of Beijing on the other, are the clearest demonstrations yet that the era of the global phone, the same device sold everywhere with the same software, is ending. The iPhone has become a device whose capabilities vary by jurisdiction, and Apple, the company that built its fortune on a single product for a single world, is being asked to make a product that is different everywhere. The question of how that reshapes the company's economics is one the launch event did not touch.

What the event did establish is that Apple has chosen its answer to the moment. The company that was famously reluctant to admit the existence of artificial intelligence has made AI the entire reason to upgrade, and the bet is total: the iPhone, the iPad, the Mac, the whole ecosystem, is being repositioned as the surface on which the intelligence runs. The bet could fail, if the features disappoint or the rollout stumbles, and it could succeed, if the integration story holds and the privacy argument draws the users it is aimed at. Either way, the event marked a change in what Apple believes it sells: not devices, but intelligence, delivered through devices. The hardware was announced on 9 September; the argument was about the decade.
