The holiest weekend of the Christian calendar arrived in the middle of the trade war, and the coincidence was worth a moment's notice: the holiday that the world's churches observe with the oldest rituals of renewal fell at the point where the year's economic argument was at its loudest. The two calendars, the sacred and the commercial, ran in parallel through the spring, and the parallel was the month's quietest lesson. The markets closed for the holiday, the negotiators paused their sessions, and the churches filled with the congregations that the festivals still gather, and the pause, brief and ritualised, was the reminder that the year's arguments, however loud, are not the whole of the year.

The rituals of the weekend were performed with the particular attention that the shared holidays preserve. The services were held in the churches that the congregations had attended through the year, the processions wound through the towns and the cities where the tradition has deep roots, and the family tables carried the foods that the regions have defined as the festival's own; the observance was the annual demonstration that the religious calendar, the oldest calendar that the world keeps, still organizes the lives that the economic calendar surrounds. The churches' attendance, reported in the dioceses' bulletins, held the levels of the recent years, and the holding was the quiet fact of the weekend.

The economic calendar, meanwhile, observed the holiday in its own way. The markets closed for the long weekend, the trading desks emptied for the days that the exchanges schedule around the festival, and the negotiators, the officials and the delegations took the pause that the holiday provided; the pause was the recognition, built into the year's schedules, that the economic machinery runs on the human calendars, and that the human calendars include the days that the world stops to observe. The trade war's documents accumulated in the inboxes, unread, and the accumulation was the weekend's contribution to the conflict: the pause that both sides took, and the resumption that both sides scheduled.

The reflection that the holiday invites is the part that the coverage of the trade war has no room for, and the reflection is worth keeping. The spring's argument about the tariffs, the rates, the prices, is a real argument with real stakes, and the stakes will be settled by the negotiations and the markets in the months ahead; the holiday's argument, the older one, is about the things that the negotiations will not settle, the renewal that the rituals enact, the community that the congregations rehearse, the hope that the festival's narrative carries. The two arguments are not opposed; they are simply different registers, and the weekend's gift is the permission to hear both, to weigh the tariffs on the weekdays and the renewal on the Sunday, and to keep the two in proportion.

The Easter of 2025 fell at the loudest point of the year's economic conflict, and the timing was the holiday's particular character. The churches filled, the tables were set, the markets paused, and the trade war waited, as all wars wait, for the calendars to resume; the resumption came on the Monday, with the negotiations and the markets taking up where they had left off, and the holiday's pause, brief as it was, had done its quiet work: the year's arguments were still there, and the year's older rhythms had held their place beside them. The trade war will be remembered for the tariffs and the talks; the Easter weekend will be remembered by the people who kept it, and the two memories will age at different rates, which is the way of all calendars.
