The Supreme Court upheld the law that would ban one of the world's most popular social apps unless its owners sold it, and the hours that followed were the strangest that the platform has known in its decade of existence. The users who had built their audiences and their friendships on the app posted their farewells in the language of a wake; the rivals dusted off their migration plans; and the company's own engineers, the people who had spent years building the service, prepared the shutdown that none of them wanted. The ruling was the end of a legal story and the beginning of a political one, and the case was never really about the app at all; it was about the terms on which the digital world is allowed to operate.

The legal reasoning of the ruling deserves to be read carefully, because it will be cited for years. The court accepted that the national security concerns that Congress had cited were real, that the app's ownership presented risks that the government could legitimately address, and that the forced sale was a remedy that the Constitution allowed the legislature to choose. The court did not endorse the law's wisdom; it endorsed its constitutionality, and the distinction is the one that the legal commentators will spend the next decade explaining. The ruling left the platform's future to the political process, which is the court's way of saying that the judges had done their part and that the rest was up to the branches that could change the law.

The practical chaos of the following hours was the part that the coverage could not keep up with. The app's service flickered and returned, the storefronts removed it and the users found workarounds, and the company's public statements, issued in the measured language that the lawyers had drafted, described a service that was being wound down with the same care that it had been run. The users' reactions, collected by the researchers who study such moments, showed the particular grief of digital displacement: the creators who lost their archive of work, the small businesses whose marketing ran through the app, the communities that had organized their entire social lives around a feed. The shutdown was a business event and a cultural event at the same time, and the two cannot be separated.

The political aftermath was the part that the ruling itself had invited. The law had been passed with bipartisan support, and the support had always been shallower than the vote totals suggested, and the weeks after the ruling showed the coalition fraying: the users who had never voted on the law discovering that they cared about it deeply, the politicians who had voted for the ban discovering that their constituents had not asked for it, and the company's new owner, whatever the sale produces, discovering that the legal risk follows the asset rather than the brand. The case will be studied by every platform and every government that watched it, and the study will conclude, with the hindsight that the ruling provides, that the app was the occasion and that the argument was about sovereignty, which is a word that the digital age has not finished defining.

The app's own history is worth remembering in the aftermath, because the history explains the panic. The platform was built by a company that the West had watched grow from a copycat into a genuine rival, and the fear that the law expressed was the fear of a technology that the country did not control, that the users loved anyway, and that the government could not regulate without also regulating the users. The shutdown, brief and confused as it was, demonstrated the answer to the fear: the users' affection was real, the dependence was real, and the law that saved the country from the platform could not save the users from the disruption. The ruling settled the legal question; the weeks that followed settled the political one, and the political one is the one that will be argued for the rest of the year.
