Once a year the roads fill with people who are not going away. At the first warm weekend of April, the intercity coaches leave early, the high-speed trains sell out by mid-morning, and the slow lanes of the highways thicken with cars that have packed a rake, a bag of fruit and a coil of joss paper into the boot. Nobody calls it a holiday, though it has the calendar of one. It is the season of Qingming, and the journey is not outward but inward, toward the place where the family came from.

The first thing one notices, driving south through the greening countryside, is how ordinary it all is. The living move through a perfectly normal Saturday. A farmer burns off a field edge. A grandmother sells vegetables by the roadside. Children ride past on the backs of bicycles, jackets slung over their shoulders because the afternoon has turned mild. And then, on the slope above the village, a thin line of people is doing something entirely different: they are bending to a mound, clearing the grass, lighting incense, speaking to someone who is not there.

The work at the grave is small and unhurried. Weeds are pulled back. The earth is patted smooth. Fresh soil is piled at the base, the way one would tidy a room for a guest. Food is laid out, tea is poured, and for a few minutes the family stands in a silence that is not awkward but full. The names on the stone are often older than the people standing in front of them. A young child who has no memory of the person buried there is still asked to bow, and does, because the bow is not about recollection. It is about the chain, about standing in the line and letting the line stand in you.

What the ritual does, quietly, is give grief a schedule. The rest of the year the dead are absent in the abstract. For one day they are present in the particular: a slope, a stone, a remembered voice, the exact way a grandfather used to cough in the morning. People do not weep their way through the visit. They talk, they light the paper, they sweep, they eat the food they brought, and on the walk back down the hill the conversation returns to the weather and the harvest and the nephew's exams. The dead are put back where they belong, in the place where they are remembered, until the same date next year.

There is something steadying in that. Modern life asks people to carry their losses as private and undated, to process them once and then move on. Qingming does the opposite. It says that remembrance is not a wound to be closed but a ritual to be repeated, a date on the calendar that the whole society keeps together. The city empties for a day, and the reason it empties is not leisure but allegiance: to the people who built the road that the coach is now travelling.

By evening the cars come back north, the graves are tidy again, the smoke has cleared, and the fields settle into the soft April dusk. The season of Qingming is short, a week or two at most. Its whole point is that it returns, every year, without fail, like the greening itself.
