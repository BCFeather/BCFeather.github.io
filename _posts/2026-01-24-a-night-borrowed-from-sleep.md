The phrase is new and the behaviour is ancient: revenge bedtime procrastination, the deliberate postponement of sleep to reclaim a few hours that the day has taken. The person who does it is not insomniac, and not indifferent to rest. They are, by their own account, owed time. The day ran long, the evening was consumed by obligations that were someone else's priorities, and so the night, the only stretch of time that belongs to no one, is paid back by being stolen from the one irreplaceable account, sleep. It is a small act of rebellion, and like most rebellions it is expensive, and the price is paid by the person who staged it.

The mathematics of the habit are straightforward and grim. The hours between eleven and one are the only hours in the day when no one can make a claim, no message is expected, no task is scheduled. Scrolling, watching, reading, anything that is chosen rather than demanded. The freedom is real; that is why it is fought for. But the account it is drawn from is the one account that cannot be topped up. The lost sleep is not recovered on the weekend in full, and the deficit compounds in the slow currency of mood, attention and immunity. The rebellion is against a day that leaves no margin, and it finances itself from the one resource the day does not control, which is precisely why it feels so necessary and costs so much.

The habit is worth examining because it is a diagnostic rather than a vice. People do not generally stay up late out of mere habit; they stay up late when the day was not their own. The prevalence of the behaviour, and its acceptance as a normal part of adult life, is a measurement of how much of the average day is spent responding to external claims: meetings, messages, obligations, the long commute, the deferred evening. The person scrolling at midnight is not irrational. They are conducting a transaction the day refused to make, exchanging tomorrow's alertness for tonight's autonomy, and the fact that the exchange is a bad one does not make the underlying shortage less real.

There is a class difference embedded in the habit, and it is rarely named. The people who can afford not to do it are the people whose days have margins, whose evenings are actually evenings, whose work does not leak into every hour. The people who do it are often the people whose days were consumed precisely because they had no power to set boundaries. Revenge bedtime procrastination is, in this reading, less a lifestyle than a small protest against the structure of a working day that has expanded to fill everything, and the protest is directed not at any person but at the schedule itself, which makes it both poignant and ineffective.

The resolution is not more discipline, which the habit's practitioners already have in exhausting abundance. It is the recovery of time earlier in the day, a margin somewhere that makes the night not the only available territory. Until that margin exists, the stolen night will continue, and the person who steals it will continue to pay for it with the only currency the day cannot touch. The rebellion is rational, which is the saddest thing about it: a perfectly reasonable response to a structure that left no reasonable alternative, and a habit that will not be broken by advice, only by the day being made more just.
