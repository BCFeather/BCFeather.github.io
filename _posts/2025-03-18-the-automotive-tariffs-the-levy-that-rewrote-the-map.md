The announcement that tariffs would be applied to imported vehicles and parts landed in the third week of March, and the announcement hit the industry that the continent's trade was built around with the force of a structural event. The automotive sector is the most integrated supply chain in the world, the industry where the parts cross the borders multiple times before the vehicle is assembled, and the application of the levies to that chain is the test that the tariff policy has been building toward; the sector's response, the analysts agree, will determine whether the tariff war's economics are manageable or transformative, and the response will be visible in the prices, the plants and the headlines of the months ahead.

The industry's structure explains why the automotive tariffs are different from the tariffs that preceded them. The vehicles and the parts that the levies target are not produced in the countries that the tariffs name; they are produced by the supply chains that the trade agreements of the past decades built, chains in which the American plants use the Canadian engines, the Mexican assemblies use the American steel, and the borders are crossed, in the industry's phrase, multiple times before the vehicle is finished. The application of the tariff at each crossing is the accumulation that the industry's models have been warning about, and the accumulation is the reason the announcement was received with the particular alarm that the sector reserved for it.

The price effects of the tariffs were the first thing the markets priced, and the pricing was the demonstration of the industry's mathematics. The average vehicle that crosses the border, the analysts calculated, would carry the additional cost that the levies impose, and the cost would be passed, in whole or in part, to the buyers, with the timing determined by the inventories and the competition; the manufacturers' share prices moved on the announcement, with the exposure to the cross-border production determining the size of the move. The consumers will feel the effect at the dealerships, and the dealers, the reports noted, were already receiving the calls from the buyers who had read the news.

The industry's response to the announcement has been conducted in the two languages that the sector speaks: the language of the lobbying and the language of the engineering. The manufacturers and the unions issued the statements about the jobs and the plants, and the statements were accompanied by the quiet work of the adjustments, the review of the sourcing, the modelling of the alternatives, the preparation of the carve-out requests that the industry's negotiators will press in the coming weeks. The adjustments are the industry's real response, and the adjustments will take months, because the supply chains that the tariffs disrupt were built over decades and cannot be rebuilt in a season; the industry will comply, adapt and negotiate, and the sequence will define the sector's year.

The politics of the automotive tariffs are the part that makes the announcement's timing significant. The industry is concentrated in the states that the electoral map hinges on, the plants employ the workers whose votes the campaigns court, and the unions, historically the allies of the tariff sceptics, have spoken this time with the careful ambiguity of organizations that see both the jobs and the prices in the same policy. The administration's bet is that the tariffs will be popular with the workers who feel the import competition and tolerated by the buyers who feel the prices; the bet will be tested by the industry's adjustments, and the testing will be conducted in the states where the plants stand. The automotive tariffs were the policy that the tariff war had been building toward, and the sector that they hit is the sector where the war's arithmetic will be settled.
