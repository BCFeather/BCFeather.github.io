They arrived in Leh on 29 April, flown in by the Indian Air Force under heavy security, and were carried through the town in a procession that drew monks, pilgrims and onlookers out onto the street. What was being escorted was not a new artwork or a visiting dignitary but bone fragments believed to be the remains of the Buddha, normally sealed behind conservation glass in the National Museum in New Delhi. From 1 May, the relics are on open public display across Ladakh for two weeks, in a sacred exposition that runs until 14 May.

For Buddhists the occasion is the religious equivalent of a state funeral, in reverse: a homecoming of something treated as living and sacred rather than as a museum object. The relics, known as *sarira-dhatu*, come from the Piprahwa stupa in Uttar Pradesh, the site identified with the ancient Shakya capital of Kapilavastu. A first excavation there in 1898, by William Claxton Peppe, uncovered a stone coffer holding five urns, and soapstone caskets inscribed in early Brahmi that linked the site to the Buddha's own clan. A second dig, between 1971 and 1977 under the Archaeological Survey of India, recovered further bone relics, and twenty of them now rest in the National Museum. On the first of May they left their case to receive the public.

The itinerary is calibrated to the region. Public veneration opened in Leh on 1 May and runs to the tenth, timed to coincide with the 2569th Buddha Purnima celebrations, with monastic prayers, lighting ceremonies and cultural performances laid on alongside. From 11 to 12 May the relics move on to the Zanskar Valley, a high, remote Buddhist community reachable only through difficult passes, before returning to Leh for enshrinement at the Dharma Centre on 13 May and departure for Delhi on 15 May. Alongside the exposition, the National Museum and local Buddhist organizations have organized conferences on Himalayan Buddhism, talks on Buddhism and science, film screenings and a photo exhibition at Leh Palace on the trans-Himalayan trade in faith.

The religious dimension is genuine, but so is the quieter, secular one. India's Ministry of Culture has been sending the national collection on the road for years: the Piprahwa relics were shown in Mongolia in 2022, relics from Sanchi travelled to Thailand in 2024, and last year relics were taken to Vietnam, to Elista in Russia and to Bhutan, with a display in Colombo following in February. Each journey is described in the same terms, as cultural exchange and the strengthening of people-to-people bonds. Whatever one makes of the framing, the pattern is clear. A country that sees itself as the birthplace of Buddhism is using the movement of its most sacred objects as a soft form of outreach, and Ladakh this month is the latest stop on a circuit that circles the Buddhist world.

The theme chosen for the exposition, *Peace in Times of Conflict*, is a pointed one for a spring when energy markets and news bulletins have been dominated by a wider war. Whether that is read as spirituality or as statecraft, it is being staged on the ground with real devotion. For the people filing past the relics in Leh this week, the diplomatic layer is beside the point. They have come to see something holy, brought, for a short season, to a town high in the mountains.
