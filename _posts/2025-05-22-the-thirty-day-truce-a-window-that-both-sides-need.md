The administration announced, in the third week of May, a thirty-day suspension of the tariffs on the largest trading partner, and the announcement, delivered from the European summit where the world's leaders were gathered, reset the trade conflict that the spring had escalated. The suspension was announced with the conditions that the year's pattern had made familiar: the partner's actions during the window would determine the extension or the reversal, and the partner's response, delivered within hours through its own channels, accepted the window while reserving its position on the terms. The truce's thirty days are the window that both sides need, and the need is the most hopeful thing that the trade conflict has produced.

The context of the announcement is the part of the story that explains its timing. The escalations of the spring had carried the tariffs to the levels that the economists had described as the worst case, and the costs had begun to show in the data that both governments read: the trade volumes down, the prices up, the growth forecasts revised; the summit that the administration was attending, the gathering of the world's leaders that the spring's schedule had fixed, provided the stage that the announcement needed and the audience that the conditions required. The timing was the diplomacy's way of saying that the costs had been weighed, and the weighing had produced the window.

The conditions of the truce are the part that the negotiators will spend the thirty days on. The suspension applies to the tariffs on the partner's goods, and the partner's countermeasures, the levies imposed in response to the spring's escalations, were acknowledged in the announcement with the expectation of the reciprocal relaxation; the negotiations that the window opens will address the structure of the trade relationship, the rates, the market access, the technology questions, and the agenda will be the map of the thirty days. The conditions are the negotiation's terms, and the terms are the starting point that the two sides' officials will now have to turn into an agreement.

The markets' response to the truce was the part of the week that the analysts will remember: the indexes rallied on the announcement, the sectors that the tariffs had hit recovering the ground that the spring had taken, and the trading desks describing the session as the release that the window's promise allowed. The rally was the market's judgement on the truce, and the judgement was the recognition that the window, however short and conditional, was the first real opening that the conflict had produced; the recognition will be tested by the thirty days' progress, and the testing will be conducted in the prices that the markets will set.

The thirty days that the truce opens will be the most consequential period of the year's trade politics. The negotiators will work through the window with the deadline providing the calendar, the two governments' domestic politics will weigh on the terms, and the markets will trade the progress and the setbacks through the sessions; the window's end will bring either the extension that the progress warrants or the resumption that the failure would restore. The truce was the window that both sides needed, and the need was the recognition that the escalation had reached its limits; the thirty days will show whether the recognition can be converted into the settlement that the year has been working toward, and the showing will be the trade story's next chapter.
