The week before the American holiday arrived with the particular rhythm that the season reserves for it, and the rhythm was the one that the calendars have fixed for generations. The travel preparations began, the airports and the highways filling with the movement that the holiday commands; the menus were planned, the turkeys ordered and the recipes consulted with the particular care that the occasion's traditions require; and the year's conversations, the ones that the families will gather for, began to be scheduled around the tables that the holiday will set. The week is the holiday's prelude, and the prelude is the season's quiet introduction.

The travel that the week carries is the part of the prelude that the coverage measures with the numbers. The millions who will move for the holiday were counted in the forecasts that the agencies issued, the peak days identified and the preparations advised, and the movement's scale is the demonstration of the holiday's place in the country's calendar; the travel is the season's logistics, and the logistics are the prelude's machinery. The numbers are the holiday's arithmetic, and the arithmetic's rehearsal is the week's work.

The menus and the preparations are the quieter part of the prelude, and the quiet is the measure of the tradition's persistence. The recipes that the families have kept, the dishes that the regions have defined, the tables that the households will set, all of it was assembled through the week with the particular care that the occasion commands; the preparations are the tradition's rehearsal, and the rehearsal is the demonstration of the holiday's continuity. The tables are the season's centre, and the centre's preparation is the week's domestic work.

The year's conversations, scheduled for the tables, are the part of the prelude that the coverage cannot reach and the families will conduct. The year's events, the trade conflicts, the wars, the elections, the prices, will be weighed around the tables that the holiday sets, with the particular mixture of the argument and the affection that the family gatherings produce; the conversations are the holiday's real content, and the content's preparation is the week's anticipation. The year will be discussed, and the discussing is the tradition that the holiday has always hosted.

The season's other markers, arriving with the week, are the ones that the holiday's approach has always carried: the charities that begin their drives, the school holidays that the calendars set, and the particular light that the late November brings, the early dusks and the decorations that the cities begin to hang. The markers are the season's setting, and the setting is the week's atmosphere; the holiday is being approached, and the approaching is the demonstration of the season's own rhythm, which runs beneath the year's events as it always has.

The holiday itself, when it arrives, will be conducted with the rituals that the week has prepared: the travel completed, the tables set, the meal shared, and the year's conversations held in the presence of the family's own history. The week before Thanksgiving arrived with the rhythm that the season reserves, and the rhythm is the prelude to the holiday's particular combination of the gratitude and the argument; the preparations were made, the tables were planned, and the year's table was set for the conversations that the holiday will host, which is the way that the season has always worked.
