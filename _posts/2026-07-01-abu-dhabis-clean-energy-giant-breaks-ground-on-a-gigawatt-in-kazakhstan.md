On 29 June, in a meeting room with a screen showing a wind farm projected behind a slab podium, officials from Abu Dhabi and southern Kazakhstan pressed a button together and declared a first sod turned. Masdar, the clean energy company owned by Abu Dhabi, had broken ground on a 1-gigawatt wind farm in the Zhambyl region, its first renewable project in Kazakhstan and one of the largest integrated wind-and-battery builds in Central Asia. The price tag is 1.4 billion dollars, and the build includes a 600-megawatt-hour battery system attached to the wind farm, plus more than 400 kilometres of overhead transmission lines to carry the power south.

The arithmetic is the kind that has become familiar along the Belt and Road's quieter stretches. When finished, the wind farm will supply enough electricity for roughly 880,000 homes in southern Kazakhstan, and the developers say it will avoid around 2.5 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. Kazakhstan has set itself a target of 15 per cent renewable electricity by 2030 and 50 per cent by 2050, and its southern regions sit on some of the steppe's best wind resource, close to the load centres of Shymkent and Almaty but short on the transmission wires that would connect them. A gigawatt of wind with a half-gigawatt-hour battery is a serious stake in that grid.

What makes the ceremony more than another groundbreaking photograph is what was signed beside it. Alongside the wind farm, Masdar and the Kazakh Ministry of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Development initialed a roadmap for the country's first round-the-clock clean energy project, designed to deliver up to 200 megawatts of baseload power for data centres. The pairing is the tell. Wind and solar are now being financed not only against decarbonization targets but against the appetite of AI infrastructure, which wants firm, predictable, daytime-and-night power and is willing to pay for it. A battery bolted to a wind farm is the cheapest answer the market has so far.

The capital route is also worth a moment. The money is Emirati, the site is Kazakh, and the co-developers include W Solar, the Qazaq Green Power arm of the Samruk-Kazyna sovereign fund, and the Kazakhstan Investment Development Fund. Gulf clean energy firms have been moving into Central Asia for years, but this is the first time a single project has tied a wind build to a roadmap for data-centre power. Kazakhstan sits between the European market and the Chinese supply chain, and it has the land, the wind and the grid interconnectors to turn that position into export-ready electrons.

There are caveats that will outlive the ribbon-cutting. Wind on the steppe is seasonal, and a 600-megawatt-hour battery, while large, is a few hours of storage against a gigawatt of turbines. Transmission build across 400 kilometres is the part of the project that usually slips, and the local supply chain for turbines is thin. The round-the-clock roadmap is, for now, a roadmap, not a construction contract.

Still, the direction is clear. Gulf capital, Central Asian wind, and the electricity needs of AI are converging on the same steppe. The button was pressed on 29 June, and by the time the turbines turn, the argument over who builds the energy transition where will have moved on.
