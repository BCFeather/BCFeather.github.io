---

On the afternoon of 19 May, a Bell 212 helicopter carrying Iran's president crashed in the fog of the Varzaqan region in East Azerbaijan province, and by the following morning the Islamic Republic had lost its president, its foreign minister and six other passengers, including the province's governor and a senior Revolutionary Guard official. President Ebrahim Raisi was returning from a dam inauguration on the border with Azerbaijan when the aircraft went down in conditions that Iranian officials later described as complex spring weather and sudden thick fog. The rescue effort, hampered by the same weather, took more than twelve hours to reach the wreckage.

The deaths, confirmed on 20 May by state media, set off a process that is at once well rehearsed and deeply uncertain. Under the Iranian constitution, the first vice president, Mohammad Mokhber, assumes the presidency on an interim basis, and a fifty-day window opens for a new presidential election. That is the legal machinery. The political reality is that power in Iran does not run through the presidency alone; it runs through the supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, and the institutions around him, and the succession of one conservative president may matter less to the system's continuity than the succession that will one day come for the supreme leader himself. Raisi was widely seen as a candidate for that office, and his removal from the field reshuffles a deck that the leadership would rather have kept untouched.

It is also impossible to separate the crash from the country's circumstances. Iran's civil aviation fleet is old and constrained by sanctions that have long limited access to spare parts, and the Bell 212 is an ageing American helicopter, bought decades ago, maintained since under the difficulty of the same sanctions. No cause has been officially established, and a military investigation has been ordered, but the discussion in the region, and in the Western press, has already turned to the question of whether the accident was avoidable, whether it was a failure of maintenance under pressure, or simply an ordinary mountain crash in bad weather. The distinction matters, because it determines whether this tragedy reads as fate or as policy.

The timing complicates everything. The crash came weeks after Iran and Israel traded direct strikes across their borders, an exchange that had pushed the region to the edge of open war and had made the presidential office newly consequential. A distracted, transitioning Iran is a less predictable Iran, and the region's powers, from Washington to Riyadh to Tel Aviv, will all be recalculating against the same uncertainty. The supreme leader's immediate response, calling for stability and confirming the interim succession, was designed to signal continuity; whether the system can deliver that continuity while the succession question is now unresolved is another matter.

What should be said plainly is that nine people died, and among them was a foreign minister who had spent his final months negotiating with neighbours, and a president who had built his reputation on a different, harsher style of politics. The mourning in Iran is genuine, whatever one thinks of the policies of the deceased. The country now faces an election it did not plan, a leadership transition it did not choose, and a fog, both literal and political, that will take a long time to clear.
