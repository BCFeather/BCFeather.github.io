East Asia is famous for its overpopulation. There is, of course, no denial that a larger population boosts business, but too many people bring about intense competitions. Job markets become fiercely cutthroat, as countless graduates chase a limited number of desirable positions, forcing young people to invest excessive time and energy in endless academic and vocational training merely to stand out. Meanwhile, resources ranging from high-quality educational institutions to affordable housing fall short of public demand, pushing living costs to a soaring level and weighing heavy pressure on ordinary families. What's worse, cutthroat rivalry often breeds impetuous social mindsets: many individuals fixate on immediate success rather than long-term self-development, ignoring personal well-being and inner peace amid the rat race.

Growing up in a densely populated region, every individual is born into an unspoken, perpetual rivalry that penetrates every stage of growth. No one is exempt from the race, and no one can afford prolonged slackness. Long-term immersion in such an environment fosters a unique collective mindset: we grow accustomed to constant self-improvement, yet also carry lingering weariness from endless comparison. We understand that diligence is not a personal choice, but a social norm woven into the fabric of East Asian life, where surplus population turns every ordinary progress into a hard-won breakthrough.

Critics often simplify East Asia's social anxiety as a direct product of large population size, arguing that dense crowds inevitably breed fierce competition and widespread involution. This judgement, however, is overly one-sided. Population density itself is never a flaw. It fuels bustling commerce, robust cultural exchange, sufficient labour supply, and vibrant social vitality—all of which have underpinned East Asia's long-standing economic prosperity. The real root of excessive competition lies not in population quantity, but in the saturation of development tracks and the homogenized growth path that the whole society follows. When countless individuals chase the same limited goals under a unified developmental logic, population advantage naturally transforms into competitive pressure. East Asian societies have long fallen into a paradox of unified, single-dimensional competition. Influenced by demographic saturation, most people follow identical life trajectories: excel in academic examinations, secure stable and high-quality careers, and pursue standardized life success. This singular evaluation system leaves little room for diversified growth and personalized development. Every year, a huge number of young people step onto the same competitive track, striving for similar resources and opportunities. The sheer volume of participants continuously raises the threshold of success, making ordinary progress increasingly difficult and turning daily efforts into repetitive, exhaustive internal consumption.

What makes East Asia's competitive atmosphere most thought-provoking is its universal, inescapable nature. It is a collective rhythm that envelops every member of society. The whole society advocates diligence, perseverance and self-struggle, conveying a consistent belief that progress comes from unremitting effort. Yet under the pressure of population saturation, even consistent effort can only maintain one's original position, rather than bringing tangible breakthroughs. Everyone keeps running, working harder, and sacrificing more time and energy, but the overall competitive landscape never improves. This is the core of East Asian involution—a tireless race with no real finish line.

Still, this rigorous, high-pressure competitive environment also forges the unique spiritual temperament of East Asian youth. Sustained rivalry teaches us to be disciplined, persistent and self-aware. We learn to resist inertia, embrace steady progress, and tolerate long-term hardship in the process of growing up. Unlike the casual growth mode in sparsely populated regions, our growth is accompanied by constant self-polishing and iterative improvement. Population pressure does not crush us; it shapes our resilience, cultivates our rigorous mindset, and lets us understand that all valuable progress requires long-term accumulation and unremitting persistence.

We do not deny the intensity of population-driven competition, nor do we reject the positive value brought by such a social atmosphere. What we pursue is to break free from blind, homogeneous involution and embrace a more diverse, rational competitive ecology. A dense population should not be a source of endless internal friction, but a driving force for diversified social progress. When society opens up more developmental paths, respects multiple forms of success, and encourages personalized growth, massive population resources can be transformed into innovative vitality, rather than mere competitive pressure.

The future of East Asian society does not have to be a zero-sum game driven by population saturation and blind competition. It can be an inclusive field where diverse growth and mutual progress coexist. Population density is an inherent feature of East Asian society, carrying both historical prosperity and contemporary challenges. Fierce competition is not an inevitable dead end, but a transitional state of social development. As society gradually optimizes its developmental structure, expands diverse growth channels, and abandons single-dimensional evaluation standards, the intense competition brought by dense population will eventually shift from internal consumption to mutual motivation. In the end, every persistent effort will be meaningful, every personalized pursuit will be respected, and the huge demographic vitality of East Asia will fully burst forth to fuel more sustainable and inclusive social progress.
