It was just after four in the morning on Saturday 5 September when the hillside above Mingkeng village let go. The village sits in Gaoping town, in Suichuan County in the city of Ji'an, in the eastern province of Jiangxi, and for days it had been taking rain that Typhoon Saudel, the eighteenth typhoon of the year, was dragging inland. By the time the mudslide reached the houses, twelve buildings had been damaged, eight of them destroyed outright and four more partially wrecked. Three people were pulled from the debris alive. By the following day the count had settled at three dead and nine missing, and search teams were still working through the mud.

The geography is the story. Saudel was never going to be a coastal spectacle. By the time its rain reached inland Jiangxi it had weakened from a typhoon into a broad, wet low-pressure system, and that is precisely what makes it dangerous. A weakening storm does not stop raining. It spreads the rain out, over days, across steep terrain that drains into narrow valleys and small villages tucked against the hills. The slopes above Mingkeng had been thoroughly wetted through. Once the pore pressure in the soil crosses a threshold, a hillside that has stood for decades can give way without any single dramatic trigger.

That slow-building risk is why autumn landslides keep catching communities off guard even when the typhoon itself is old news. The public attention goes to the wind at the coast. The inland counties get days of steady rain that look routine on a weather chart and then, one wet night, stop being routine. The residents of Mingkeng were asleep when the slope moved, which is the worst case for this kind of event and the one that early-warning systems are designed to prevent.

The response, as reported by local officials and state broadcasters, followed the familiar shape of these disasters. Residents were relocated out of harm's way, fire and rescue crews were sent in to dig through the debris, and authorities warned of secondary landslides as the ground stayed saturated. Nine people were still missing at the time of writing, which means the final toll is not yet known. These numbers move as the search goes on, and they are reported as provisional for good reason.

The harder question, the one that does not have a press conference attached to it, is how much of this is new. Autumn rainfall in southern China is not itself unusual. What has changed is the backdrop: a warmer atmosphere carries more water, and typhoons that weaken over land still carry a great deal of it. Villages like Mingkeng, built against slopes that have been stable for living memory, were sited for the climate of the twentieth century. A hillside that rarely moved before now has to be watched.

Nobody is suggesting that small villages on steep ground can all be moved. The practical work is narrower and less dramatic: better rainfall thresholds for evacuation, better mapping of which slopes are already primed, and a willingness to act on a forecast rather than on the noise of a collapsing hillside. For now, the search continues in Jiangxi, and the village is holding its breath the way a village does when it is still waiting for the last names.
